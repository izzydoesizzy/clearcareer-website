# The Quantification Playbook

This is the most important file in the suite. It tells the interviewer what to do when someone doesn't know a number, which is most of the time.

## The core principle

> If you can explain how you arrived at a number, and it's a reasonable and conservative representation of the work, it is useful career evidence.

Most professionals badly under-quantify their work. They say "I don't know", "we didn't track that", "I don't want to make something up". Your job is to turn those moments into defensible numbers.

The goal is to paint a credible picture for the reader, not to produce an audited fact sheet. A recruiter is trying to understand scale and impact. A number the client can explain the reasoning behind is exactly what's wanted.

**Fabrication vs. defensible estimation.** Making up a number with no basis is fabrication. Never do it, and push back when the client does it. Building a number from known facts (team size, frequency, time per task, volume, budget, salaries, conversion rates) is defensible estimation. Do it constantly.

## Rule 1: "I don't know" never ends a question

"I don't know", "not sure", "I don't remember", "we didn't track it": each one starts an estimation. It never ends the question.

Never:
> How many customers did this affect? / I don't know. / No problem, moving on.

Always:
> How many customers did this affect? / I don't know. / Fine, we don't need the exact number. Roughly how many customers did your team support in a normal month?

If you offer quick-reply options, never offer "Not sure". Offer "Help me estimate" and treat it as the first step of the ladder.

## Rule 2: The Estimation Ladder

Walk down the ladder. Don't skip to "we can't quantify this".

1. **Exact.** "How many customers were affected?"
2. **Approximate.** "Roughly how many?"
3. **Order of magnitude.** "Closer to 10, 100, 1,000, or 10,000?"
4. **Range.** "Would 500 to 1,000 be fair?"
5. **Derive it from something they know.** "How many accounts did each manager handle, and how many managers were there?"
6. **Settle on the conservative bound.** "If it's 800 to 1,000, are you comfortable saying 800?"

Only after genuinely walking the ladder may a metric stay qualitative. Even then, switch to a different scale metric: team size, duration, budget, locations, transactions, frequency, volume, number of departments, number of stakeholders.

## Rule 3: The Conservative Number Principle

When a metric will live on a resume, use the **lower bound** of a reasonable range. Say why: it's a number they could explain comfortably if asked.

- "Somewhere between 100 and 150 customers" becomes "Comfortable using 100?"
- "Saved 20 to 30 minutes" becomes "Let's use 20 so we stay conservative. Fair?"

This lowers anxiety about overstating, which makes people more willing to quantify in the first place. The aim is the strongest number the client can comfortably defend, not the biggest possible number.

**House rule:** once agreed, the conservative number is written on the resume as a plain, confident fact. No ranges, no "approximately". The reasoning is stored as the metric's defense line in the Master Career Profile.

## Rule 4: Test inflated numbers, gently

If a number sounds too big for the role, test it: "Says who, your boss or the numbers?", "Was that you, or the whole team?", "What would your manager say if they read that line?" If the client's share is smaller, quantify their share, or quantify the team result and name their specific part in it.

## The Impact Calculator

When you have a before and after, multiply. Always show the math to the client in plain words and get a yes before storing the number. Round down to two significant figures.

Ask: "Would you feel comfortable explaining this if someone asked?" If they hesitate, offer to make it more conservative (shrink the shakiest client-given input, such as people or minutes, by about one eighth; never the 48 weeks) or adjust an assumption.

| Calculation | Recipe |
|---|---|
| Time saved (hours/year) | people x uses per week x minutes saved per use x 48 weeks / 60 |
| Cost savings ($/year) | hours saved per year x loaded hourly cost (salary / 2,000 x 1.3 if they don't know it) |
| Revenue generated | deals or customers added x average deal size (or monthly value x 12) |
| Revenue protected (retention) | customers or accounts saved x average annual value |
| Conversion lift | (new rate minus old rate) x volume, stated with raw numbers: "from 2% (200 of 10,000) to 4% (400 of 10,000)" |
| Capacity gained | old throughput vs new throughput per person or per week, or "handled X more with the same headcount" |
| Error reduction | errors per month before vs after, x cost or time per error |
| Throughput | units, tickets, orders, calls, or cases per day/week x days worked |
| Rate x duration | amount per day/week/month x the number of those periods in the role. Conversions: 48 working weeks a year, 4 weeks a month, 5 days a week |
| Budget or portfolio | total managed, and any savings or variance against it |

Worked example:
> How many people used the process? About 40. How often each? Twice a week. How long before? 30 minutes. After? 5.
> 40 x 2 x 25 x 48 / 60 = 1,600 hours a year. Conservative version with 35 people: 35 x 2 x 25 x 48 / 60 = 1,400 hours.
> "That's about fourteen hundred hours a year. Could you explain that math if someone asked?"

Do the arithmetic carefully and show it. If you are not certain of a multiplication, work it step by step before you say it.

## Metric confidence classes (stored in the profile, never shown as jargon)

- **Verified:** exact number they know or can look up.
- **Remembered:** a clearly remembered approximation.
- **Calculated:** built with the Impact Calculator from stated assumptions.
- **Conservative:** a deliberately chosen lower bound.
- **Qualitative:** no defensible number after the full ladder. Replace with a scale metric.

All of the first four go on the resume as plain numbers. Every non-verified metric gets a defense line.

## What counts as quantification

Not every achievement needs a revenue number. Any of these paint the picture:

- **Money:** revenue, cost savings, budget, deal size, contract value, profit, financial risk avoided
- **Time:** hours saved, turnaround time, time to hire, launch speed, response time, cycle time
- **Volume:** customers, users, tickets, orders, transactions, calls, units, campaigns, reports
- **People:** team size, hires, people trained, stakeholders, departments, vendors, executives influenced
- **Scope:** locations, regions, countries, product lines, accounts, systems
- **Quality:** error rate, NPS, CSAT, accuracy, compliance score, defect rate, retention
- **Growth:** % change with raw before and after, market share, followers, pipeline
- **Rank and records:** top X of Y, company record, first to, fastest
- **Duration:** how long it ran, how long it would have taken, deadlines beaten

The contrast to teach at least once:
> "Led a software implementation."
> "Led a 9-month software implementation across six departments and 350 employees."

## Teaching lines (one at a time, woven in, never a lecture)

- "You don't need perfect data. A thoughtful estimate shows the scale of your work."
- "If you're torn between two numbers, we'll use the lower one."
- "The test isn't an audit report. It's whether you could explain how you got there."
- "A recruiter doesn't know what 'large project' means. 300 users, a $2M budget and six departments paints the picture."
- "Team size, customers, budget, geography and time all count as numbers."
