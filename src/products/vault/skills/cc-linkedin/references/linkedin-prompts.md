# LinkedIn prompts

Ten prompts for your LinkedIn profile, your outreach and your posts. Use them with the cc-linkedin skill in Claude, or paste them into any AI chat tool. Swap everything in [square brackets] for your own details. The samples inside the prompts are fictional and only there to show the structure, so never copy their numbers.

## Write Discoverable LinkedIn Headlines

**What it does:** Writes 10 LinkedIn headlines that combine your title, your focus and your best numbers so recruiters find you and click.
**When to use it:** When your headline is only your job title, or says "Open to work" and nothing else.

### Step 1: Write 10 headlines

~~~text
Job title: [Job title]
Industry: [Industry]
My best quantified achievements: [Your top 2 to 3 achievements]
Tools, specialties or clients I can name (optional): [Tools or specialties]

Write 10 high-impact LinkedIn headlines for a [Job title] in the [Industry] industry, in the structure and style of the examples below.

These headlines should make my profile hard for recruiters to skip. Focus on impact, the value I bring, and my strongest quantified achievements. Use only the facts I've given you. Don't invent numbers, tools or client names. Keep each headline under 220 characters, which is LinkedIn's limit.

Output a 2-column table: # | LinkedIn headline. Follow the structure and style of the examples, with my details in place of theirs. Write all 10.
~~~

## LinkedIn Boolean Search String Generator

**What it does:** Asks you a few questions, one at a time, and builds a LinkedIn Boolean search string you can paste straight into the search bar.
**When to use it:** When LinkedIn job searches show you too many wrong roles, or you want a sharper job alert.

### Step 1: Build your search string

~~~text
Help me build a LinkedIn Boolean search string for job searches (it can also work for finding people). Ask me the questions below one at a time. After each answer, show me how it fits into the string. At the end, give me the complete string in a code block I can copy and paste into LinkedIn.

QUESTIONS
1. Job titles or keywords: Ask for the job titles or keywords I want. If I give more than one, join them with OR and wrap each phrase in quotes for exact matches.
   Example: "software engineer" OR "product manager"
2. Skills or expertise: Ask for the skills the roles must mention. Join them with OR, then use AND to add them to the titles or keywords.
   Example: ("Java" OR "Python") AND "cloud computing"
3. Titles or keywords to exclude: Ask if I want to exclude any. Put NOT before each one.
   Example: NOT "intern" NOT "junior"
4. Location: Ask for my preferred locations. Join several with OR.
   Example: "Toronto" OR "Vancouver"
5. Companies: Ask if I want to target specific companies, and join them with OR. Ask if I want to exclude any, and use NOT for those.
   Example: ("Company A" OR "Company B") NOT "Company C"
6. Other filters (optional): Ask about education, industry, certifications or anything else to add.

When you have all my answers, put the final string together, use parentheses so the logic is clear, and give it to me in a code block, ready to copy. Write AND, OR and NOT in capital letters, because LinkedIn only reads them that way. Then add two short tips: LinkedIn also has separate Location and Company filters if the string gets long, and I can save the search as a job alert.
~~~

**Replace:** Nothing. It asks you questions one at a time.


## LinkedIn Connection Request Scripts

**What it does:** Writes short connection requests and follow-up messages for the 5 most common job search networking situations.
**When to use it:** When you want to reach recruiters, hiring managers or old colleagues and don't know what to say.

### Step 1: Write your scripts

~~~text
Act as a LinkedIn networking coach who has helped many professionals build networks that lead to job opportunities. You understand why people accept connection requests, why they ignore them, and how to open a door without being pushy.

ABOUT ME
My resume or background: [Paste your resume or a short summary]
Target roles: [Target roles]
Target industries and location: [Target industries and location]
Target companies (if any): [Target companies]

CHARACTER LIMIT (THE MOST IMPORTANT RULE)
LinkedIn connection notes have a hard limit of 300 characters, including spaces, and some accounts get a lower limit. Write every connection request in 200 characters or fewer so it fits everywhere. Count carefully and show the count after each one, like (168 characters).
For scale: "Hi Alex, I'm a senior project manager exploring roles in fintech. Your post on payment fraud caught my eye. I'd love to connect and learn from your experience." is 159 characters.

Follow-up messages (sent after they accept) have no tight limit, but keep them to 100 to 200 words.

WHAT TO WRITE
1. A quick usage guide (5 to 7 bullets)
2. Five scenarios, each with 3 connection request versions and one follow-up message
3. Personalization tips
4. A do's and don'ts table

QUICK USAGE GUIDE
Include points like these:
- Fill in every [bracket] before sending.
- Check the character count before sending.
- Send a small batch each day (10 to 15 is plenty) instead of dozens at once.
- Try mid-week mornings in their time zone.
- Wait 1 to 2 days after they accept before sending the follow-up.
- Never send without changing the [Specific detail] part. Generic notes get ignored.
- Track every message in a simple spreadsheet.

SCENARIO 1: A RECRUITER AT A TARGET COMPANY
Context: I've found a recruiter at one of my target companies and want to get on their radar.
Connection request, 3 versions:
- Version A, the direct approach: say I'm interested in [Role type] roles at [Company] and name one relevant strength. Structure: "Hi [First name], I'm a [Job title] with [X] years in [Industry]. I see [Company] is growing its [Department] team. I'd love to connect and share how my [Key skill] could help."
- Version B, the compliment approach: mention something the recruiter posted or the company is known for, then tie it to my interest.
- Version C, the mutual connection approach: mention a shared connection, group or school, if there is one.
Follow-up message (100 to 200 words) that:
- thanks them for connecting
- gives a 2 to 3 sentence pitch of my value
- names a specific role or team, if I know one
- links to my resume or LinkedIn profile
- ends with a soft ask (a conversation, not a job) and a suggested timeframe

SCENARIO 2: A HIRING MANAGER
Context: I've found the likely hiring manager for a role, or a leader of a team I want to join.
Connection request, 3 versions:
- Version A, research-backed: mention a specific project, article or win of theirs and tie it to my experience.
- Version B, value first: lead with what I bring. Mention one specific achievement that fits their team's work.
- Version C, a thoughtful question: ask a real question about their team's work, direction or a challenge in the field.
Follow-up message (100 to 200 words) that:
- refers back to the connection request
- shares 1 to 2 achievements from my resume that fit their team's work
- shows real interest in their team's mission or recent work
- does NOT ask for a job, and asks for a 15-minute conversation instead
- suggests a fair exchange: "I'd love to hear about [Topic] and share my take on [Relevant area]."

SCENARIO 3: A MUTUAL CONNECTION WHO CAN INTRODUCE ME
Context: Someone in my network knows a decision-maker at a target company. If we're already connected, skip to the message.
Connection request, 3 versions:
- Version A, warm reference: name the mutual connection and how we're linked (former colleagues, same school, same group).
- Version B, shared interest: mention a professional interest we share.
- Version C, event or content: mention a post they shared or an event we both attended.
Message (100 to 200 words) that:
- sets the context ("I noticed we're both connected to [Mutual connection]...")
- clearly and politely asks for an introduction to one specific person
- makes yes easy with a short forwardable blurb they can copy
- offers to return the favour
- gives an easy out ("Totally understand if the timing isn't right.")
Forwardable blurb (50 to 75 words), for example:
"Hi [Their contact's first name], I'd like to introduce you to [Your name], a [Job title] with [X] years in [Industry]. They're exploring [Role type] roles, and I thought you two should talk given your work at [Company]. I'll let [Your first name] take it from here."

SCENARIO 4: SOMEONE I MET AT AN EVENT OR WEBINAR
Context: I went to a conference, webinar, meetup or networking event and want to follow up with someone I met or a speaker I heard.
Connection request, 3 versions:
- Version A, specific reference: name the event and something specific from our chat or their talk.
- Version B, the takeaway: mention an insight from the event that stuck with me.
- Version C, continued conversation: pick up a conversation we started but didn't finish.
Follow-up message (100 to 200 words) that:
- names the event and date
- recalls a specific moment or topic
- shares a resource or thought that keeps the conversation going
- suggests a next step (coffee chat, video call, swapping notes)
- stays warm and friendly, not transactional

SCENARIO 5: A FORMER COLLEAGUE
Context: I'm reconnecting with someone I used to work with. If we're already connected, skip to the message.
Connection request, 3 versions:
- Version A, the catch-up: warm and casual, mentions our shared history.
- Version B, congratulations: mentions a recent win, promotion or milestone on their profile.
- Version C, nostalgia: mentions a shared project, team or memorable moment.
Message (100 to 200 words) that:
- opens with a real personal touch, not an ask
- briefly updates them on my career since we worked together
- mentions I'm exploring roles in [Target area]
- asks if they're open to a quick catch-up call, with a specific time suggestion
- does NOT ask for a job, and asks for advice, their view, or "Who should I be talking to?"

PERSONALIZATION TIPS (5 to 7)
Cover things like: check their recent posts and mention one; mention shared groups, schools, certifications or volunteer work; match their formality (tech is often more casual, finance more formal); mention a shared language, culture or place for international outreach; lead with a real comment if they post often; skip emoji with recruiters and hiring managers unless their profile is casual; adjust my value pitch to what each person cares about.

DO'S AND DON'TS TABLE
Do | Don't
Personalize every message | Send the same note to 50 people
Mention something specific about them | Open with "I'm looking for a job"
Stay under 200 characters | Push right up to the limit
Wait 1 to 2 days to follow up | Send the follow-up the moment they accept
Offer value before asking | Open with a request
Use their first name | Use "Dear Sir/Madam"
Ask for a conversation | Say "I'd love to pick your brain"
Track every message | Send and forget

FORMATTING
- Separate each scenario with a line.
- Put every variable in square brackets, like [First name].
- Show the character count after each connection request.
- Fill in as many details as you can from my background.
- Format follow-up messages as block quotes so they're easy to copy.

AVOID
- Connection requests over the limit (the most important rule)
- "Pick your brain"
- Opening with "I hope this message finds you well" or "I know you're busy"
- Cramming my whole career history into a request
- Being vague about why I'm connecting
- Asking for a job in the request itself
- LinkedIn's default "I'd like to add you to my network" note
- Links or attachments in connection requests
- Made-up details about the person. Leave a [Specific detail] bracket for me to fill in.

TONE
Warm, professional and curious. Like a real person reaching out, not a sales pitch and not a desperate job seeker. Someone who does their homework, has something to offer, and is worth knowing. Confident, never arrogant.
~~~

**Replace:** [Paste your resume or a short summary], [Target roles], [Target industries and location], [Target companies]

## LinkedIn Comment Responses

**What it does:** Builds you a commenting plan for your job search: who to comment on, 10 reusable comment templates, and a 3-touch path from comment to connection.
**When to use it:** When you want to get noticed on LinkedIn without writing your own posts yet.

### Step 1: Build your commenting plan

~~~text
Act as a LinkedIn strategist and career coach. Write me a commenting strategy guide for my job search, with comment templates I can reuse.

ABOUT ME
Target roles: [Target roles]
Target industry: [Target industry]
My background and expertise: [Paste your resume or a short summary]
Target companies (if any): [Target companies]

Write the guide in second person ("you"), warm and practical. Include every section below.

SECTION 1: WHY COMMENTING MATTERS
3 to 4 sentences. Cover:
- Comments can show up in front of the poster's network, not only yours.
- Thoughtful comments lead to profile views, including from hiring managers and recruiters.
- Commenting takes less effort and feels less risky than posting, so it's a great place to start.
- Showing up again and again builds familiarity ("I keep seeing your name"), which warms up outreach later.

SECTION 2: WHO TO COMMENT ON
A priority list, tailored to my target industry and roles:
Tier 1, direct targets (3 to 5 comments a week):
- Hiring managers at target companies in [Target industry]
- Recruiters who post [Target roles] jobs
- Leaders at my target companies
- People in [Target roles] jobs who share what their work is like
Tier 2, industry voices (2 to 3 a week):
- Respected voices in [Target industry]
- Newsletter writers and creators in my field
- Conference speakers and panel organizers in my specialty
- Leaders of professional associations in my field
Tier 3, broad visibility (1 to 2 a week):
- Career coaches and recruiters with big followings
- Authors of trending posts on topics in my field
- Journalists and outlets covering [Target industry]
Describe 3 to 5 types of accounts that fit my field, with search terms I can use on LinkedIn to find them. Don't invent names of real people.

SECTION 3: COMMENT TEMPLATES
Give me 10 templates across 4 categories. For each one, include when to use it, the template with [bracket] placeholders, a filled-in example based on my field and background, and a length guide.

Category A: Agree and add (3 templates). Use when I agree with the post and can add a supporting view or example.
A1, add a data point or example:
"This rings true. In my experience [working in your field or leading your function], I've seen [a specific observation that supports the post]. One thing I'd add: [an extra insight]."
A2, share a short story:
"[Poster's name], this is spot on. I saw this firsthand when [a 1 to 2 sentence story from your career]. The lesson that stuck with me was [a takeaway that connects to the post]. Thanks for putting this into words."
A3, add industry context:
"This is especially true in [Your industry] right now. [1 to 2 sentences on why the point matters in your field.] Great perspective, [Poster's name]."

Category B: Respectful disagreement or nuance (2 templates). Use when I see a different angle. Always frame it as adding a view, never attacking.
B1, "yes, and":
"Interesting take, [Poster's name]. I agree with [the part you agree with], though I've found that [your different view]. In [your field], [brief evidence from your experience]. Curious if others have seen the same."
B2, it depends:
"This is great advice for [situation A]. I wonder if it plays out differently in [situation B]? In my experience with [relevant context], [your observation]. Curious what you think, [Poster's name]."

Category C: Ask a smart question (3 templates). Use to start a conversation with a respected voice. Questions get replies, and replies create threads both networks can see.
C1, dig deeper:
"Really useful, [Poster's name]. I'm curious: how do you see this playing out for [a group you care about]? I've been thinking about [a related angle] and would love your take."
C2, applying it:
"Great framework. How would you apply this in [a specific context from your field]? I've been trying [a related approach] and wondering if [your specific question]."
C3, looking ahead:
"Fascinating perspective. Where do you see this heading in the next 2 to 3 years for [your industry or function]? I've noticed [a trend you've seen] and I'm curious if you're seeing it too."

Category D: Share relevant experience (2 templates). Use when the topic is right in my area of expertise. These comments make me look like a peer, not a spectator.
D1, here's how I handled it:
~~~
