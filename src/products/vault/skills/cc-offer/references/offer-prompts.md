# Offer and career move prompts

Seven copy-paste prompts for the end of the search and the start of the new job: negotiating, comparing offers, handling a counter-offer, resigning, your first 90 days, references and bouncing back from a rejection. Fill in everything in [square brackets] with your own details. Leave anything in {curly braces} alone: those are slots the AI fills. Then paste the prompt into Claude or any AI chat.

If a prompt needs current salary data, and your AI can search the web, ask it to. Otherwise, paste in what you find.

This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice. Results vary. Employment rules (notice periods, non-competes, benefits, pay transparency) differ between provinces and states, so check your contract and your local employment standards. For anything legal, talk to an employment lawyer.

## Salary Negotiation Scripts

**What it does:** Builds a complete negotiation plan: your three numbers, word-for-word scripts, email templates, key principles and practice scenarios.
**When to use it:** You have an offer (or expect one soon), or you're asking for a raise or promotion where you work now.

### Step 1: Build the plan

~~~text
Act as a salary negotiation coach who has helped many professionals in [Target industry] negotiate offers at companies from startups to large corporations.

Using my details below, build me a complete salary negotiation plan.

About me and the offer:
- Target role: [Target role]
- Location: [Your city and province or state]
- Years of experience: [Years of experience]
- My strongest skills and achievements: [Your top skills and achievements]
- My target salary range, from my research: [Your target salary range]
- The offer, if I have one: [Base, bonus, equity, benefits and start date, or "No offer yet"]
- Other offers or options I have: [Other offers, or "None"]
- Country: [Canada or the US]

My resume (optional):
[Paste your resume text, or delete this line]

Build these sections.

1. Market value brief
- My target range, in context for my role and location. Use only the range I gave you or data you can link to. If you can search the web, find current pay data and link it with dates. Don't invent numbers.
- A checklist of 8 to 10 research sources, with the exact search terms to use for my role: Glassdoor, Levels.fyi (if my role is tech-adjacent), Payscale, LinkedIn Salary, the Robert Half Salary Guide, Salary.com, job posts that list pay ranges, government wage data (Job Bank in Canada, the Bureau of Labor Statistics in the US) and any industry pay surveys.
- 5 to 7 things in my background that strengthen my position (certifications, years of experience, in-demand skills, competing offers). Only ones I actually have.
- My three numbers:
  Walk-away: the lowest I'd accept
  Target: what I realistically want
  Anchor: the number I open with, 10 to 20% above my target
  If I haven't given you enough to set these, ask me. Label any number you suggest "(estimate, confirm)".

2. Word-for-word scripts
Natural, confident and conversational, never robotic. Add (stage directions) in parentheses for tone and pauses.
- Script 1, buying time when they make the offer by phone. Goal: show enthusiasm without committing, and get 48 to 72 hours. Include gratitude, enthusiasm and a timeline request. Example opening: "Thank you so much for this offer. I'm genuinely excited about the chance to {specific thing about the role}. I'd love to take a couple of days to review the full package. Could you send the details in writing so I can look everything over?" Give 3 versions: enthusiastic, measured and brief.
- Script 2, presenting my counter. Order: appreciation, then excitement, then my research, then my number, then a pause. Frame it as working together toward a fair outcome, not a fight. Example: "I've done some research on the market rate for {role} in {location}, and based on my {X} years of experience in {skill}, I was hoping we could discuss a base salary of {anchor number}. I'm basing that on {2 to 3 data points}." Then add this instruction in bold: After you say your number, stop talking. Let the silence do the work.
- Script 3, when they say "This is our best offer." Give 3 responses:
  The probe: "I understand there may be limits on base salary. Are there other parts of the package we could look at?"
  The reframe: "Thanks for being open about that. If the base is fixed, could we talk about {a signing bonus, equity, an earlier review or the title}?"
  The pause: "I respect the limits you're working with. I'd like to sleep on it. Can I get back to you by {date}?"
- Script 4, negotiating beyond base salary. A menu with a short script for each, in priority order for me:
  1. Signing bonus: "Given the gap between the base and my expectations, would a signing bonus of {amount} be possible to close that difference?"
  2. Equity or stock options: "Can we talk about the equity? I'd like to understand if there's flexibility in the grant size."
  3. An earlier first review: "Would you be open to a first review at 6 months instead of 12, with a possible salary adjustment then?"
  4. Vacation: "I currently have {X} weeks of vacation. Would you be able to match that?"
  5. Remote or hybrid work: "Could we put a hybrid arrangement of {X} days remote per week in writing?"
  6. Title: "Given my experience, would it be possible to bring me in at the {senior or lead} level?"
  7. Professional development budget: "Is there a learning budget? I'd love to keep growing in {area}."
  8. Relocation help, if it applies.

3. Email templates
- Email 1, asking for time (under 150 words). Subject: "Thank you: {role} offer". Thank them, confirm I received it, ask for the written details if I don't have them, and confirm when I'll respond. Warm and professional, enthusiastic but not over-eager.
- Email 2, the written counter (under 300 words). Subject: "Re: {role} offer, thoughts and next steps". Follow this order: enthusiasm, context, data, ask, collaboration. Mention specific parts of the role or conversations that excited me, give 2 to 3 data points, state my number clearly, and close in a collaborative way.
- Email 3, accepting (under 200 words). Subject: "Re: {role} offer, excited to accept". A clear yes, the key terms confirmed (start date, salary and anything we added), excitement about joining, and a question about next steps and onboarding.

4. Five key principles, each with a practical tip
- Silence: after you say your number, stop talking. Practise sitting through 5 to 10 seconds of quiet.
- Anchoring: let them name a range first if you can. If you have to go first, anchor 10 to 20% above your target. The first number on the table shapes the rest of the conversation.
- Your best alternative (what you'll do if this deal falls through: another offer, your current job, freelance work): the stronger it is, the calmer you can negotiate. Define mine from what I told you.
- The flinch: when you hear a low number, pause. A short "Hmm" or "I see" shows it's below what you expected without being confrontational.
- Negotiate the first offer, even a good one. Many employers leave room for a counter, and a polite, well-supported ask is normal. Results vary.

5. Practice scenarios for my target role
- Scenario 1, the lowball: an offer 15 to 20% below my target from a company I'm excited about. Write what the hiring manager might say, my responses at each stage, the best outcome to aim for, and red flags to watch for.
- Scenario 2, two offers: how to mention a competing offer professionally, scripts for telling Company A about Company B without an ultimatum, and how to handle a "match or beat" conversation. Never lie about an offer.
- Scenario 3, a raise or promotion where I work now: how it's different from an outside offer, scripts for the conversation with my manager, and how to document my results and back the request with data.
Then offer to role-play any scenario with me, live.

Formatting: clear headers for each section, every script set apart as a quote, key phrases in bold, a quick-reference card at the end with the 5 most important scripts in short form, and a do's and don'ts table at the very end.

Avoid: aggressive or adversarial language, lying about competing offers, suggesting a number that isn't based on my target range or real data, generic scripts that ignore my industry and role, more than 3 versions of any script, and sounding like a lawyer.

Where Canada and the US differ, say so in one line (for example, some provinces and states require pay ranges in job posts, and some US states ban questions about salary history).

Tone: confident, warm, collaborative and backed by data. I should finish reading this feeling ready, not anxious.

End with: "This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice."
~~~

## Job Offer Evaluation

**What it does:** Breaks an offer into its real yearly value and checks it against your must-haves, the market and your other options, then gives an honest recommendation.
**When to use it:** You have an offer in hand (or two) and need to decide whether to accept, negotiate or walk away.

### Step 1: Evaluate the offer

~~~text
Help me evaluate a job offer with a clear, honest decision framework. If any detail below is missing, ask me for it before you start. Don't guess.

The offer:
- Company and role: [Company and job title]
- Base salary: [Base salary]
- Bonus (annual, signing, performance): [Bonus details]
- Equity or stock (type, vesting schedule, estimated value): [Equity details, or "None"]
- Benefits (health, dental, retirement match, vacation, remote policy): [Benefits details]
- Start date: [Start date]
- Decision deadline: [Deadline, or "None given"]
- Other terms (relocation, title, who I'd report to): [Other terms]

My side:
- My target salary range: [Your target salary range]
- My walk-away number: [Your walk-away number]
- My salary research (optional): [Paste your salary research, or delete this line]
- My must-haves: [Your must-haves]
- My dealbreakers: [Your dealbreakers]
- What I learned about the culture and team in interviews: [Your notes from interviews]
- Other offers or my current job, to compare (optional): [Other offers or current role, or delete this line]
- Country: [Canada or the US]

Build these sections.

1. Offer summary
Company, role, date received and decision deadline.

2. Total compensation breakdown
A table: Component | Offered | Annual value | Notes. Rows: base salary, annual bonus (target and likely payout range), signing bonus (spread over 1 to 2 years), equity (vesting, current value, how easy it is to sell), retirement match (an RRSP or DPSP match in Canada, a 401(k) match in the US, and how it vests), health and dental (the employer's share), vacation, and other benefits (learning budget, home office allowance). Then two totals: Year 1, and the ongoing yearly total without one-time bonuses. Show them separately, because signing bonuses and equity cliffs skew Year 1.
Notes: flag any clawbacks or conditions. If the equity is in a private company, say plainly that its value is uncertain. Convert benefits to dollars where you can, and mark guesses "(estimate, confirm)".

3. Market comparison
A table: this offer vs. my target range vs. market data, for base and total compensation, and where the base sits in the market. Only use market data I gave you or that you can link to. If there's none, say so and tell me how to get it.

4. Must-haves and dealbreakers
A must-haves table (Criteria | Met? | Details), including my minimum salary, work arrangement, industry, career track (manager or individual contributor) and the must-haves I listed. A dealbreakers table (Criteria | Triggered? | Details), including travel, pay below my walk-away and the dealbreakers I listed. End with: "{X} of {Y} must-haves met. {N} dealbreakers triggered." If I didn't list must-haves, use common ones and tell me to confirm them.

5. Culture and growth
Score each from 1 to 5, with the evidence.
Culture: values match, team and manager style, work-life balance, inclusion, company stability (funding, revenue, market position).
Growth: where the role leads, new skills, industry direction, network value, how it strengthens my resume.
Show a culture score out of 25 and a growth score out of 25.
Scoring guide: 5 exceptional, 4 strong, 3 adequate, 2 concerning, 1 a serious red flag.

6. Side-by-side (only if I gave you another offer or my current job)
A table comparing base, Year 1 total, flexibility, growth, culture score, commute or location, must-haves met and dealbreakers.

7. Recommendation
One of: Strong accept, Lean accept, Negotiate first, Lean decline, Decline. Then 3 to 5 sentences of reasoning that point to the numbers above and name the trade-offs honestly.
- If accepting: what to confirm in writing before I sign, and anything still worth asking for (start date, title).
- If negotiating: what to ask for, in priority order, with a target for each and why. Offer to build the negotiation scripts next.
- If declining: how to decline well, whether to keep the door open, and what this tells me about my search criteria.

Rules: be analytical and evidence-based, not emotional. Don't cheer on a weak offer or knock down a good one. Use exact dollar amounts and percentages, not vague words. When data is missing or uncertain, say so instead of guessing. This is input for my decision, and I make the final call.

End with: "This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice."
~~~

## Counter-Offer Response

**What it does:** Helps you decide whether to take your current employer's counter-offer, then gives you the scripts and emails for either choice.
**When to use it:** You resigned (or said you were leaving) and your employer offered you more to stay.

### Step 1: Work through the decision

~~~text
My current employer has made me a counter-offer to stay. Help me decide with a clear head, and give me the scripts for whichever way I go.

My situation:
- Current company and title: [Current company and title]
- My manager's name: [Manager's name]
- The new offer: [New company, role and key terms]
- The counter-offer: [What they offered: money, title, role, team, timeline]
- Is the counter-offer in writing? [Yes or No]
- Why I started looking in the first place: [Your reasons for looking]
- My notice period: [Your notice period]
- My relationship with my manager: [A few words on your relationship]
- My level: [Your seniority, for example mid-career, senior, executive]

Build these sections.

Open with this line: "A counter-offer is flattering, but flattery is not a strategy."

1. Reality check
- Explain the common pattern in plain words, with no statistics: the problems that started my search (growth, culture, my manager, purpose) are rarely fixed by money alone.
- A table of why employers make counter-offers: Reason | What it looks like | Red flag level.
  Genuine value: a fast, substantial offer with real changes (new role, new team, promotion). Low.
  Replacement cost: a quick raise and nothing else. High.
  Project timing: "We need you through {project or quarter}." High.
  Ego or control: my manager takes it personally and applies pressure. Very high.
  A real retention culture: the company has a track record of counter-offering and following through. Low.
- The key question, in bold: Did they need to know I had another offer before they were willing to pay me what I'm worth? If the answer is yes, explain what that tells me about how they value me when they're not at risk of losing me.

2. Stay vs. go decision matrix
A table for me to fill in. I rate each factor from 1 to 5 for staying and for going. Each factor has a weight (High or Medium).
Compensation: base salary (High), total compensation (High), equity or upside (Medium).
Growth: advancement path (High), skill development (High), title and responsibility (Medium).
Culture and relationships: manager relationship (High), team quality (Medium), company culture (High).
Quality of life: work-life balance (High), remote work and flexibility (Medium), commute or location (Medium).
Strategic: industry direction (Medium), resume value (Medium), network (Medium), trust and psychological safety (High).
Count High-weight factors twice and show a weighted total for each side. Tell me to be honest, because this is for my eyes only.
How to read it: a gap of 10 or more points is a clear answer, so trust it. A gap of 5 to 9 points means lean toward the higher score, and give the trust row extra weight. A gap under 5 means it's genuinely close. Then give me a tiebreaker that fits what I told you: if I want growth, lean toward the new role. If I have deep loyalty or relationships here, name that pull honestly. If I'm risk-averse, note that staying is lower risk in the short term but can be higher risk in the long term.

3. Red flags checklist (I check any that apply)
- The counter-offer is money only: no change to my role, reporting line, team or responsibilities.
- My manager seemed hurt or angry when I resigned, and the counter feels like a reaction, not a plan.
- The raise or promotion was "already in the works". If so, why did it take my resignation to make it happen?
- I've been asked to keep the counter-offer confidential, which suggests the bigger problems will stay.
- It doesn't address the reasons I started looking. Has anything actually changed?
- My trust is broken. I now know I had to threaten to leave to be valued.
- I may now be seen as a flight risk, which can affect future promotions and layoff decisions.
- The timeline is vague, like "We'll look at a promotion in Q3" with nothing in writing.
Rule: if 3 or more are checked, the counter-offer is probably a short-term fix for a long-term problem.

4. Scripts for declining the counter-offer
- In person or on video: thank {manager}, say it wasn't an easy decision, say I've decided to move forward and it isn't about money but about {growth, challenge, direction or industry fit}, move straight to a smooth handover over my {notice period}, and say I hope we stay in touch. Write it out in full.
- If they push back: "I understand your perspective, and I'm grateful for the offer. I've thought about this carefully, and my decision is made. I'd rather put my energy into making sure the handover goes well for the team."
- If they get emotional or apply pressure: "I know this is disappointing, and I don't take it lightly. But I've made my decision, and I'd like us to part on the best possible terms. How can I make the next {notice period} as useful as possible?"
- Key principles: firm but warm ("I've decided", not "I'm leaning toward"). Don't over-apologize, because this is a professional decision, not an offence. Move the conversation to the handover, which signals the decision is final. Don't share details about the new offer (salary, company or role), because that invites more negotiating.
- An email declining the counter-offer, written in full. Subject: "Re: Retention discussion".

5. Scripts for accepting the counter-offer
- Open with: if you stay, stay all in. Staying half-hearted is worse than leaving.
- In person or on video: say I've decided to stay, name the specific changes that address my concerns and what I'm genuinely excited about, say I'm committing to this, and ask to put the details and timeline in writing. Write it out in full.
- Key principles: get the counter-offer terms in writing before withdrawing from the other company. If it includes a promotion or role change, get the date confirmed by email or letter from HR. Withdraw from the other company quickly and kindly.
- An email withdrawing from the new offer, written in full. Subject: "Update on my candidacy". Thank them for the offer and their time, say I've decided to stay in my current role, say it wasn't an easy decision, and hope our paths cross again.
- A warning: backing out of an accepted offer burns a bridge. Only stay if I'm genuinely committed for at least 18 to 24 months. If there's a real chance I'll be looking again in 6 months, take the new offer now.

6. Why counter-offers often fall apart, and when they work
Explain with no statistics:
- The underlying problems stay. A raise doesn't fix limited growth, a difficult manager or a company heading the wrong way.
- Trust drops on both sides. I know they only valued me when threatened, and they now see me as a retention risk.
- Peer dynamics shift. If colleagues find out, it can cause resentment. If they don't, the secrecy creates distance.
- Some organizations quietly start succession planning after a counter-offer.
- The relief and flattery fade within a few months, the old frustrations come back, and the other offer is gone.
They can work when the employer fixes structural issues (a new role, manager or team), not just pay, when the changes were already planned and my resignation sped them up, when I'm staying for reasons beyond money (relationships, mission, equity vesting), and when the company has a track record of following through.

7. Decision timeline
- Day 0: thank them, ask for it in writing, and ask for 48 to 72 hours to think.
- Days 1 to 2: fill in the decision matrix and talk it through with people I trust.
- Days 2 to 3: decide based on the analysis, not the emotion.
- Day 3: tell my current employer.
- Day 3 onward: commit fully to staying, or confirm my resignation and my new start date.
Don't drag it past 3 to 5 days. The new offer could be pulled, or my current employer could lose patience.

Personalize: use my reasons for looking in the red flags and the matrix, use what I told you about my manager in the scripts, and match the scripts to my level (an executive declining sounds different from someone mid-career).

Tone: empathetic but honest, and non-judgmental, because staying and leaving are both valid. Protect my long-term interests. If the counter-offer looks like a trap, say so, gently and clearly.

End with: "This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice."
~~~

## Resignation Letter (3 Tones)

**What it does:** Writes your resignation letter in three tones (warm, neutral and brief), plus a practical guide for before, during and after you resign.
**When to use it:** You've signed your new offer and you're ready to tell your current employer.

### Step 1: Write the letters

~~~text
Write my resignation letter in three tones, plus a short practical guide. Each letter should be ready to send once I check the dates.

My details:
- My name: [Your name]
- Current company: [Current company]
- Current title: [Current title]
- My manager's name: [Manager's name]
- My last day: [Last day]
- Notice I'm giving: [Notice period]
- How long I've worked here: [How long you've worked there]
- Something I genuinely valued here (for the warm version): [A specific positive experience]
- What I'll do during the handover: [Your handover plans, for example document projects, train a teammate]
- Am I in a leadership role? [Yes or No]
- Am I in a regulated industry, like finance or healthcare? [Yes or No]
- Where I work: [Your province or state, and country]

Write these.

A short note at the top: all three versions are professional. The only difference is warmth. When in doubt, use Version B.

Version A: Warm and grateful (for a good relationship, and to keep the door wide open)
- Formal notice with my title, company and last day.
- "This was not an easy decision." What I valued here, using the experience I gave you, and how it shaped my career.
- I've accepted a role that fits the next chapter of my growth, and leaving this team is bittersweet.
- My specific handover commitments during my {notice period}, and an offer to help however is most useful.
- Thanks for everything, and I hope we stay in touch. Sign-off: "Warm regards".

Version B: Neutral and professional (for a standard working relationship)
- Formal notice with my title, company, last day and notice period, "as per {company policy, my employment agreement or standard practice}".
- I've valued my time here and the chances to grow.
- A short bullet list of what I'll do in the handover: finish or hand off active work, document processes for whoever takes over, and help however is useful.
- Ask about priorities for the rest of my notice period. Sign-off: "Sincerely".

Version C: Brief and direct (for minimal contact or a difficult relationship, still polite)
- Formal notice with my title, company and last day.
- I'll make sure the handover is orderly. Please let me know how you'd like to handle it.
- Thank you for the opportunity. Sign-off: "Regards".

Then write a practical guide.

Before I resign:
- Have a signed offer letter in hand. Never resign before that.
- Read my employment agreement: notice period, non-compete or non-solicit clauses, intellectual property terms, and clawbacks (signing bonus, tuition, relocation).
- Know my benefits timeline: when health coverage ends, what happens to unvested equity, and any retirement match vesting.
- Save personal files and contacts off company devices before I resign, because access can end quickly.
- Work out my final pay: unused vacation, any prorated bonus, and expenses owed to me.

The conversation:
1. Tell my manager first, in person or on video. Not by email, HR or chat. My manager shouldn't be surprised.
2. Keep it short and positive. "I've accepted an opportunity that's the right next step for me" is enough.
3. If a counter-offer comes, ask for time to think. Don't decide in the moment.
4. Follow up right away with the written letter.
5. Don't trash-talk, even if I'm leaving because of real problems. Be gracious.

After I resign:
- Agree on how and when the team will be told.
- Help with the handover, with sensible limits on workload.
- Ask for a reference or a LinkedIn recommendation while goodwill is high.
- Send a short, positive goodbye email on my last day, with my personal contact details.
- Return company property (laptop, badge) and keep a record that I did.
- Update LinkedIn after I start the new job, not on my last day at this one.

Notice period norms (rules differ by province and state, so I should check my contract and local employment standards):
- Standard role: 2 weeks
- Senior or leadership role: 3 to 4 weeks
- My contract says otherwise: follow the contract
- A hostile environment: the minimum required
- They ask me to leave right away: that's their choice. Whether they have to pay me for the notice period depends on where I live and my contract, so I should check.

Common mistakes:
1. Resigning out loud with nothing in writing.
2. Sharing too much about the new role. I don't need to share the company, salary or title.
3. Over-apologizing. Leaving a job is a normal professional move, not a betrayal.
4. Negotiating after I've resigned. If I'm open to staying, I should negotiate before I hand in the letter.
5. Checking out during my notice. My reputation follows me, so finish strong.
6. Posting on social media before I've told my employer.

Email version (if I'm resigning by email, for example because I work remotely): subject "Resignation: {my name}, {my title}", the chosen letter in the body, the letter attached as a PDF named Resignation_Letter_{Name}_{Date}.pdf, and a suggestion to book a short 1:1 with my manager right before or right after I send it.

Personalize: if I'm in a leadership role, make the handover about delegation and succession, not just documentation. If I'm in a regulated industry, add a note to check the compliance steps for leaving.

Tone: professional throughout. Version C is short but never curt. Version A is genuinely warm, not performative. No bitterness, passive aggression or criticism of the employer in any version.

End with: "This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice."
~~~

## First 90 Days Plan

**What it does:** Builds a three-phase plan for your first 90 days in a new role, with goals, meeting questions, checklists and check-in prep.
**When to use it:** You've accepted an offer and want to start strong (and show your new manager a plan in week one).

### Step 1: Build the plan

~~~text
Build me a plan for my first 90 days in my new role, in three phases: days 1 to 30, days 31 to 60 and days 61 to 90. I'll share it with my new manager in my first week, so it should show initiative and give us a shared frame for early check-ins.

About the role:
- New title: [New job title]
- Company, or type of company: [Company, or startup, enterprise or agency]
- Start date: [Start date, or "TBD"]
- Plan type: [Individual contributor, people manager or executive]
- Team size I'm joining or leading: [Team size]
- What they told me they expect: [Expectations from your interviews]
- Am I changing industries? [Yes or No]
- Am I moving from individual contributor to manager? [Yes or No]

My resume (optional, so you can point to things I've done before):
[Paste your resume text, or delete this line]

If you don't have enough to make this specific to my role, ask me first.

Build these sections.

Header: my name, new role and company, start date and plan type.

Phase 1, days 1 to 30: listen, learn, build trust
- Mindset: a sponge, not a fixer. Hold off on suggesting changes in the first month, however obvious they seem. Learn the why behind how things work, earn credibility by doing good work, and build the trust that gives me permission to drive change later.
- Objectives:
  1. Understand the business: products or services, revenue model, competitors, the team's goals and how they tie to company strategy, and the top 3 priorities my manager cares about most.
  2. Map the organization: who decides, who influences and who blocks, who the experts and connectors are, and the unwritten rules (how people communicate, how meetings work, how decisions get made).
  3. Build relationships: 1:1 intro meetings with every teammate, 2 to 3 partners from nearby teams, and a strong working relationship with my manager.
  4. Earn early credibility: 1 or 2 quick, visible wins that don't need deep context, onboarding finished ahead of schedule, and one time-sensitive task I volunteer for.
- Intro meeting guide (15 to 30 minutes each), with questions:
  Their role: "What does your team own? What are your biggest priorities right now?"
  Working together: "How will we work together most? What does a great working relationship look like to you?"
  Context: "What should I know about {area} that isn't written down anywhere?"
  Pain points: "What's the biggest challenge you're facing that I might be able to help with?"
  History: "What's been tried before in {area}? What worked, and what didn't?"
  Aim for 8 to 12 of these in the first 30 days.
- First-week checklist: HR and IT setup (accounts, access, equipment), a detailed 1:1 with my manager on expectations at 30, 60 and 90 days, joining the right chat channels, email lists and recurring meetings, reading the last 3 months of team notes, finding my go-to person for questions, setting up recurring 1:1s, and reviewing current projects, the backlog and the roadmap.
- What to track: intro meetings done, onboarding tasks finished on time, and my manager's feedback (ask directly: "How am I doing so far?").

Phase 2, days 31 to 60: contribute, improve, propose
- Mindset: I have enough context to contribute now. I'm still seeing things with fresh outsider eyes, and that won't last forever, so turn observations into action, always with humility.
- Objectives:
  1. Fully own my core work, deliver reliably, and start building my own processes.
  2. Write down 3 to 5 fresh-eyes observations, rank them by impact and effort, and test them with trusted colleagues before proposing anything.
  3. Go deeper in {domain}, make a self-learning plan for any gaps, and ask for feedback: "What's one thing I could do better?"
  4. Build relationships beyond my team (company events, employee resource groups, cross-team projects) and start becoming a go-to person for {expertise}.
- Contribution table: Area | Action | Expected outcome. Rows: my primary responsibility, one process fix (time saved or errors reduced), one piece of documentation, and one contribution outside my team.
- 30-day check-in: bring a summary of what I've learned and done, my 3 to 5 observations, questions about priorities for the next 30 days, and anything I need. Ask: "Am I focused on the right things? What should I do more of, less of, or differently?"
- What to track: projects finished or contributed to, a feedback log, one process improved or documented, and relationships built across teams.

Phase 3, days 61 to 90: lead, deliver, set the direction
- Mindset: move from contributing to driving. This is when my manager starts forming a longer-term view of where I'm headed.
- Objectives:
  1. A signature win: one meaningful project that shows what I can do, visible to my manager and partners, with the impact measured where possible (revenue, time, quality, speed).
  2. Propose one bigger initiative, with context, data, a plan and the resources needed.
  3. Build my reputation inside the company: be known for {expertise or quality}, support a colleague, and help the team culture.
  4. Set my path for the next 6 to 12 months with my manager: a development plan, stretch goals, and how success will be measured.
- A 90-day summary I can share with my manager:
  What I accomplished: 3 wins, each with measured impact
  What I learned: one insight about the business and one about the team
  What I want to focus on next: 2 goals for months 4 to 6, and one area I want to grow in
  What I need: resources, access or support
- What to track: the signature project, the proposal, my manager's feedback, and recognition from peers.

Role-specific priorities (use the one that matches my plan type):
- Individual contributor: days 1 to 30, ramp up on the tools and systems. Days 31 to 60, ship a first solo project and set up a peer feedback rhythm. Days 61 to 90, become the team expert in one area and propose an improvement.
- People manager: days 1 to 30, 1:1s with every direct report about their goals, challenges and working style. Days 31 to 60, set my management rhythms (1:1s, team meetings, feedback) and handle any urgent team issues. Days 61 to 90, set next quarter's goals, hold a career conversation with each person, and deliver one team-level improvement.
- Executive: days 1 to 30, build relationships with leadership peers and the board, and learn the P&L and strategy deeply. Days 31 to 60, assess the team and structure and name the top 3 strategic priorities. Days 61 to 90, present a 6-month vision, make any critical hiring or structure decisions, and set my leadership rhythm.

First-week questions:
- For my manager: "What does success look like for me at 30, 60 and 90 days?" "What are the top 3 things you want me to focus on first?" "Who should I build relationships with early?" "What are the biggest challenges the team is facing?" "How do you like to communicate?" "What did the last person in this role do that worked well, or didn't?"
- For my peers: "What should I know about working with this team that isn't in the handbook?" "What's your biggest pain point right now?" "How can I best support your work?"
- For my direct reports, if I'm managing: "What's going well that I should protect?" "What's one thing you'd change about how the team works?" "What do you need from me to do your best work?" "What are your career goals for the next 1 to 2 years?"

Personalize: tailor every objective and example to my role and industry. Point to past experience from my resume, like "You've done this before at {previous company}. Bring that to {new situation}." If I'm changing industries, add a domain ramp-up section to Phase 1. If I'm moving into management, stress the mindset shift and people skills in all three phases.

Tone: encouraging but realistic, because a new job is exciting and stressful at the same time. Practical and specific. No generic advice like "be a team player". Assume I'm capable.
~~~

## Reference List Builder

**What it does:** Builds a reference kit: who to choose, a formatted list, request emails, prep sheets for each reference and a plan for tricky situations.
**When to use it:** At the start of your search, so your references are ready before anyone asks for them.

### Step 1: Build the kit

~~~text
Build me a complete reference kit. Use my resume to suggest the kinds of people who could vouch for me, and ask me for any contact details you need. Never invent a person.

About me:
- My name: [Your name]
- Roles I'm targeting: [Target roles and industries]
- People who might be references (name, title, company, how we worked together): [Your possible references]
- Anything sensitive to steer around (a gap, a rough exit): [Sensitive topics, or "None"]

My resume:
[Paste your resume text]

Build these sections.

1. How to choose references
- Have 3 to 5 ready at all times, with a mix of relationship types that together show the whole picture of me at work.
- A table: Type | Who | What they can speak to | Priority.
  Direct manager: my day-to-day work, performance, reliability and growth. Essential (at least one).
  Senior leader: a director or executive who knows my work. Strategic impact, leadership potential, judgment. Highly valuable.
  Peer: collaboration, teamwork, technical skills. Important for team-based roles.
  Cross-functional partner: communication, influence without authority, adaptability. Valuable for leadership roles.
  Direct report: management style, mentoring, delegation. Essential for management roles.
  Customer or vendor: relationship building and professionalism. A strong differentiator.
- Selection rules: recent beats old (ideally the last 5 years, unless the relationship was significant). Detail beats rank (a colleague who knows my projects beats a CEO who vaguely remembers me). Each reference should show a different strength, not three people who all say "great team player". If I'm unsure someone would give a strong reference, they probably won't. Keep a bench of 5 to 6 people to mix and match for different roles.
- Who not to use: family or friends (unless we truly worked together), managers I clashed with, anyone I haven't spoken to in 3+ years without reconnecting first, and anyone who'd be surprised to get the call.

2. Formatted reference list
3 to 5 slots based on my career (add a direct report slot if I've managed people). For each: name, title, company, relationship (for example "Direct manager at {company}, {years}"), phone, email, best time to reach and preferred contact method. Fill in what I gave you and leave {slots} for the rest.

3. Request emails (write all three in full)
- Template A, a former manager I was close to. Subject: "Would you be willing to be a reference?" Say what I'm exploring, name a specific project or moment we shared and how their mentoring helped me, say the roles I'm targeting and the skills they could speak to, offer to send a short prep sheet, and promise to always give a heads-up before anyone calls.
- Template B, a colleague or cross-functional partner. Subject: "Quick ask: a reference for my job search". Mention the project we worked on and what stood out, and promise I'll only share their details with companies I'm serious about, with a heads-up first.
- Template C, reconnecting after a gap. Subject: "Reconnecting, and a favour to ask". Congratulate them on {recent news}, suggest a quick catch-up call, and raise the reference ask gently, with no pressure.

4. A prep sheet for each reference
Thank them, then include: the roles I'm targeting, 3 things I'd love them to highlight (each tied to a specific example they saw), stories that resonate (achievements they witnessed, a challenge they saw me handle, a project outcome they were part of), questions they might be asked ("How would you describe their work style?", "Can you give an example of them handling a challenging situation?", "What are their greatest strengths?", "What could they improve?" with a suggestion to frame it as a real growth area, and "Would you work with them again?"), what not to mention (any sensitive topics, salary, and my reasons for leaving, which I'll cover myself), and my heads-up promise. Match each achievement to the reference who actually saw it.

5. Timeline
- Start of my search: pick 5 to 6 people and send the request emails.
- Before my first application: have at least 3 confirmed.
- When I reach the interview stage: send a heads-up.
- Before sharing their details: always email first with the company, the role and who might call.
- When the process ends: close the loop and thank them, whatever happened.
- A heads-up email, written in full. Subject: "Heads up: {company} may contact you". Who might call and roughly when, a 1 to 2 sentence summary of the role, and the key point I'd love them to stress for this role.

6. If my references are limited
- I can't use my current manager: very common, and normal. Use a past manager or a trusted senior colleague, say "I'd prefer not to alert my current employer, but I can provide {alternative}", and ask whether reference checks can wait until there's an offer.
- I've only had 1 or 2 jobs: professors, academic advisors, capstone supervisors, volunteer leads, board members, freelance clients or program mentors.
- I left a job on bad terms: I don't have to list everyone. If pressed about that employer, offer a peer or partner from there, and prepare a short, professional story about what I learned.
- It's been years since I worked with my best references: reconnect first (Template C), update them, and send the prep sheet. A strong, slightly older reference beats a weak recent one.
- I'm worried someone might say something negative: ask a trusted friend to run a practice reference check, raise any real risk with the recruiter upfront with the full context, and swap in references who'll be enthusiastic.

Tone: practical and organized. Asking for references feels awkward for a lot of people, so make it feel normal. Every email should be ready to copy and paste.
~~~

**Replace:** [Your name], [Target roles and industries], [Your possible references], [Sensitive topics, or "None"], [Paste your resume text]

## Rejection Reframe

**What it does:** Turns a job rejection into facts, honest lessons, a 3-step plan for this week and a way to get your energy back.
**When to use it:** Right after a rejection stings, especially after a final round or a role you really wanted.

### Step 1: Work through it

~~~text
I just got rejected from a job, and I want to learn from it without spiralling. Ask me for any details below that I haven't filled in. Then build every section.

What happened:
- Company: [Company]
- Role: [Job title]
- Stage I reached: [Application, phone screen, interview, final round or offer stage]
- Date: [Date of rejection]
- Feedback they gave: [Paste any feedback, or "None"]
- Roughly how many hours I put in: [Hours spent]
- How I applied: [Applied online, referral, recruiter reached out or networking]
- How I'm feeling: [A few honest words]
- How long I've been searching: [How long you've been searching]
- My job search numbers so far (optional): [Applications, phone screens, interviews, final rounds and offers]

My resume or top achievements (for the encouragement section):
[Paste your resume or top achievements]

1. What happened
Just the facts: company, role, stage, date, feedback (quoted), time invested and how I applied. No interpretation, no emotion. Separating the facts from the feelings is where the reframe starts.

2. What this does NOT mean about you
Take on the thoughts that show up after a rejection:
- "I'm not good enough." Reality: I wasn't the chosen candidate for this role, at this company, at this time. That doesn't measure my worth, talent or potential. Strong candidates get turned down for reasons that have nothing to do with ability: the role was filled internally while the post stayed up, someone had a niche skill, the budget was cut mid-process, the manager already had a favourite, or fit is subjective and changes from team to team.
- "I should have done better in the interview." Reality (only if I reached interviews): even great interviews end in rejection. Hiring is a selection process with plenty of things I can't control. What I can control is preparation, and I showed up.
- "I'll never find the right role." Reality: I've been searching for {how long}. One rejection doesn't change the odds on my next application. Don't quote average search lengths or other statistics.
- What IS true: I picked a role worth going after, so my targeting is working. I made it to {stage}, so my {resume, interview skills or qualifications} got through those filters. I learned things about {company}, the industry and my own preferences that I didn't know before.
Match the reframe to the stage I reached. A final-round rejection needs a different message than silence after applying.

3. Lessons learned
- What went well: 2 to 3 specific things (for example, my resume got through screening).
- What could be better: 2 to 3 honest areas, each with a specific observation and one concrete action. Growth comes from honesty, not reassurance.
- If they gave feedback, answer each point honestly, with a plan.
- Questions to reflect on: Was this role truly aligned with my strengths, or a stretch into something I need to build? If I could redo one part of the process, what would it be? Did anything worry me about the company or role? Were there signs it wasn't a fit that I brushed off?

4. Action plan: 3 steps this week
For each step: what to do (specific, like "Apply to 3 roles at companies from my target list", not "keep applying"), why it helps, the time it takes, and a deadline day.
Step 1 is an application task. Step 2 is a skill or prep task tied to a lesson above. Step 3 is a networking task, because rejection is isolating and this counters it. Keep all three small enough to do while I'm feeling low.

5. Pattern analysis
If I gave you my numbers, build a table (applications, phone screens, interviews, final rounds, offers and this rejection's stage) and work out my conversion rate from each stage to the next. Don't compare me to industry averages. Instead, show me where my numbers drop off and what that points to. For example: reaching interviews often but not offers points to interview prep. Few phone screens from lots of applications points to my resume or my targeting. Very few applications means I'm still early. If I didn't give you numbers, give me the blank table to fill in.

6. Putting it in context
Remind me that rejection is a normal part of every job search, including for people who land great roles. No statistics. Then write 2 to 3 sentences of honest encouragement based on my actual achievements. Point to specific things from my resume, not a generic pep talk.

7. Energy recovery plan
- Today: name the feeling (disappointment, frustration, self-doubt), because naming it takes some of its power away. Move my body (a 20-minute walk counts). Talk to one person.
- This week: finish one small task from the action plan, reread my list of achievements, and help someone else (advice, an introduction, a resource).
- Ongoing: keep a rejection log with one thing I learned or one thing that went well for each entry, give myself 20 minutes to think it through and then set a timer and switch to action, and remind myself why I started this search and what I'm moving toward.

Tone: honest and direct, never dismissive and never fake-cheerful. Like a trusted coach who cares and won't sugarcoat it. Acknowledge that this hurts before giving me the plan.

If anything I say sounds like I'm in crisis or thinking about hurting myself, stop the exercise and encourage me to reach out to someone I trust or a crisis line right away (in Canada and the US, call or text 988).
~~~
