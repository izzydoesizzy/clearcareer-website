# Method: find the person, find the email, send, follow up

This is the method behind every email this skill writes. Read it when the user doesn't have a person or an email address yet, wants to plan a week of outreach, or asks how the approach works. Explain it in plain, friendly steps. The user does the searching. You guide them, help them read what they find, and write the emails.

The core belief: don't just apply and wait. Find the people who hire for the role, find their email addresses, send short and specific emails, and follow up.

**1. Pick targets.**
Work in batches of 10 companies. Mix companies with a posted role and companies with no posting where the user knows the team they'd join. For each company, note the role or team. That tells you who to look for.

**2. Find the person who hires for the role.**
Look for the person the role reports to, usually one or two levels above it.

- Company under about 50 people: the founder, the CEO or the head of the function.
- Mid-size company: the director or manager who leads the team.
- Large company: the manager of the specific team. A recruiter is a second contact, not the first.

Where the user can look:

- **LinkedIn:** open the company page (not a group or showcase page), click People, type the function plus a level ("finance manager", "head of design"), and add a location filter if the role is local.
- **The job post:** some LinkedIn postings show "Meet the hiring team". Many descriptions say "reporting to the [Title]".
- **The company website:** Team, About or Leadership pages.
- **Google:** site:linkedin.com/in "[Company]" "[Title]" searches LinkedIn profiles through Google.

Pick two people per company: the likely hiring manager and a backup (a team lead, or someone already in the role). If the user can't tell who the hiring manager is, use the "Who's the right person?" template to ask someone close to the team.

How you help: suggest the two or three most likely titles for the hiring manager, based on the posting (a Senior Accountant usually reports to an Accounting Manager or a Controller, for example). Never claim to know who a specific person is unless the user found them, or you found them through a search and said where.

**3. Find the email address.**
Work through these in order and stop at the first solid address.

1. **Published:** LinkedIn "Contact info", their posts, a personal website, a conference speaker bio, a podcast page, or a press release with a media contact.
2. **The company pattern:** find any one real email at the same domain (a press release, a job post listing a recruiter's email, a blog author page) and apply its format to the person's name. Common patterns:
   - first.last@company.com
   - first@company.com
   - firstlast@company.com
   - flast@company.com (first initial, then last name)
   - firstl@company.com (first name, then last initial)
   - first_last@company.com
3. **A finder or verifier tool:** many have a free tier with a few searches a month. The user enters the company website and the person's name. A verifier checks whether the mail server accepts an address. Never tell the user they need a paid tool.
4. **Free checks:** search the address in quotes. In Gmail, type it in the To field and hover over it: a name or photo suggests it's real (no pop-up doesn't prove it's wrong).

How to read the results:

- Valid or verified: send.
- Accept-all or risky: the server accepts everything, so the tool can't tell. Send to the best guess and try the next pattern if it bounces.
- Bounced: try the next pattern, or reach the person on LinkedIn.

One person, one best guess, one email. Never suggest sending the same email to several guessed addresses at once.

How you help: list the likely addresses in order and label each one "(likely pattern, verify before sending)". You can't check whether an address works, so never imply that you did.

**4. Write.**
Two ways in:

1. **The advice ask:** 15 minutes of their perspective on the team, the role or how to break in. Low pressure, and it often leads to introductions. Best for career changers and companies with no posting.
2. **Direct interest:** point to a posted job, show a fit with its top priority, and ask for a short call. Best when there's a posting and a strong match.

Every email: under 120 words, opens with something specific and true about them, one or two wins, one small ask, a short plain subject line, plain text, no attachments.

**5. Send.**
From the user's personal email, with their full name and a simple signature (name, phone, LinkedIn URL). Tuesday to Thursday mornings in the recipient's time zone is a sensible default, not a rule. Five to 10 a day at most, each one written for that person. If there's a posting, the user applies through the careers page too and says so in the email. Log every send in the tracker.

**6. Follow up.**

- Follow-up 1: 4 to 6 business days later, in the same thread, 2 or 3 sentences.
- Follow-up 2: about a week after that. Add something useful, offer an easier yes (two questions by email), or close the loop politely.
- Then stop. Three touches in total. Stop at once if anyone asks.
- Applications: one follow-up, 7 to 10 business days after applying.
- Interviews: a thank-you within 24 hours. If they gave a date and it passes, one check-in 2 business days later.

**7. Track.**
A simple spreadsheet: Company, Person, Title, Email, How I found it, Sent, Follow-up 1, Follow-up 2, Reply?, Next step. Some email add-ons show when an email was opened. Treat that as a hint, not proof: some email apps open messages automatically for privacy, and some block tracking. If an email bounces or gets nothing after both follow-ups, the person may have left or be on leave. Suggest someone else on the team.

**If someone asks "How did you get my email?"**
It rarely comes up. If it does, the user answers honestly and briefly ("I worked it out from the company's email format because I wanted to reach you directly") and offers to use another channel. If the person asks for another channel, the user switches and doesn't email them again.

**One-to-one only.**
These are personal emails, not marketing. Canada's CASL and the US CAN-SPAM Act are mainly about commercial and bulk email, but the user should follow their spirit: one person at a time, about their work, with a real name and real contact details, never added to a list, and stop if asked. This is general guidance, not legal advice.

**Common worries, and short answers.**

- "It feels weird to email a stranger." It does at first. By the tenth email it feels normal.
- "Why would they help me?" Many people like helping when the ask is small and specific. A hiring manager with an open role wants good people to find them.
- "What if they ignore me?" Silence usually means busy, not no. Follow up twice, then move on.
- "Am I going around the recruiter?" Not if the user applies through the careers page too and says so.

## Find the person who hires for the role

**What it does:** Turns a job posting into the likely titles of the hiring manager and a few searches to find them.
**When to use it:** You know the company and the role, but not who to email.

### Step 1: Get the likely titles and searches

~~~text
Here is a job posting I'm interested in at [Company]:

[Paste the job posting]

Based on this posting, list the 3 most likely job titles of the person this role reports to. Then give me:
1. Two keyword searches I can type into the People tab on [Company]'s LinkedIn page.
2. Two Google searches in this format: site:linkedin.com/in "[Company]" "[Title]"

Don't guess any names. I'll find the person myself.
~~~

**Replace:** [Company], [Paste the job posting]

## Work out an email address

**What it does:** Lists the most likely email addresses for one person, in order, based on a real address from the same company.
**When to use it:** You have the person's name and one real email address from someone else at the company.

### Step 1: List the likely addresses

~~~text
I want to email [First name] [Last name] at [Company]. The company's email domain is [domain.com].

Here is one real email address I found for someone else at the company: [example@domain.com]

Using that format, give me the most likely address for [First name] [Last name]. Then list 3 backup patterns in order of likelihood. Label every address "likely, not verified". Don't invent anything about the person.
~~~

**Replace:** [First name], [Last name], [Company], [domain.com], [example@domain.com]

### Step 2: Check before you send

~~~text
Before sending, check the top address:
1. Search it in quotes on Google.
2. Run it through a free email verifier.
3. In Gmail, type it into the To field and hover over it.

Valid or verified: send it.
Accept-all or risky: send to your best guess, and try the next pattern if it bounces.
Bounced: try the next pattern, or message them on LinkedIn instead.

Send to one address at a time. Never send the same email to several guesses at once.
~~~

**Replace:** Nothing. This step is a checklist.

## Plan a week of outreach

**What it does:** Builds a 7-day outreach plan from a list of target companies.
**When to use it:** You have a list of companies and want to turn it into sent emails this week.

### Step 1: Build the plan

~~~text
Here are my target companies and roles:
[List of companies and roles]

My background in two lines: [Your title, years of experience, and one win]

Build me a 7-day outreach plan using this method:
Day 1: Confirm 10 targets and set up a tracker (Company, Person, Title, Email, How I found it, Sent, Follow-up 1, Follow-up 2, Reply?, Next step).
Day 2: Find 2 people per company: the likely hiring manager and a backup.
Day 3: Work out emails for the top 10 people and note one specific, true hook for each.
Day 4: Send 5 emails. Apply through the careers page first if there's a posting.
Day 5: Send 5 more, then send short LinkedIn connection requests to the same people.
Day 6: Reply to anyone who answered, book calls, and prepare a 30-second intro, 2 wins and 3 questions.
Day 7: Set follow-up dates (4 to 6 business days after each send) and pick the next 10 companies.

For each day, give me a short checklist specific to my list. Keep every email under 120 words. Don't invent names, email addresses or facts about any person or company.
~~~

**Replace:** [List of companies and roles], [Your title, years of experience, and one win]
