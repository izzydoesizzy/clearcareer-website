# Tricky moments

Prompts for exploding offers, lowballs, competing offers, pushback, raises and promotions, counter-offers from your current employer, and the fear that asking will cost you the offer. This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice. Results vary.

## Handle an exploding offer

**What it does:** Writes a calm request for more time, and helps you decide if they say no.
**When to use it:** When the deadline is very short, or they ask you to accept on the call.

### Step 1: Ask for more time

~~~text
Act as my salary negotiation coach. [Company] offered me the [Job title] role and wants an answer by [Their deadline]. I'd like until [The date I want].

Am I waiting on another process? [Yes, with the date I expect to hear, or no]

Write:
1. A short request for more time (phone and email versions, two or three sentences each). Thank them and stay enthusiastic.
2. If I said yes above, a version that mentions I'm wrapping up another process, without naming the company. If I said no, don't mention other processes at all.
3. A reply for "Can you accept today?" on the call that asks for the offer in writing and names when I'll respond.

Then give me three questions to answer if they won't extend, so I can decide on my numbers and must-haves instead of the clock.
~~~

**Replace:** [Company], [Job title], [Their deadline], [The date I want], [Yes, with the date I expect to hear, or no]

## Respond to a lowball offer

**What it does:** Helps you respond to an offer well below the range you discussed, without panic or offence.
**When to use it:** When the offer is far below the range you talked about or below your walk-away.

### Step 1: Respond calmly

~~~text
Act as my salary negotiation coach. I got a low offer.

The offer: $[Their number] base, plus [Rest of the package]
The range we discussed earlier: $[Low] to $[High] (or "we never discussed a range")
My three numbers: anchor $[Anchor], target $[Target], walk-away $[Walk-away]
Market data I found (real, with sources): [Market data]

Write:
1. A reply that thanks them, points out the gap from the range we discussed (or the market data), and asks how they landed on the number. Two to four sentences.
2. If the offer is below my walk-away: a reply that says honestly I can't say yes at this number and names my target (never my walk-away) as what I'd need, open to base or other parts of the package.
3. One line on what their answer to "how did you land on that number?" might tell me (a different level, a band, a mistake) and which counter to use next.

Keep it calm and kind. No ultimatums. Don't invent market data.
~~~

**Replace:** [Their number], [Rest of the package], [Low], [High], [Anchor], [Target], [Walk-away], [Market data]

## Use a competing offer honestly

**What it does:** Writes the words to mention a real competing offer, or to ask a company to speed up.
**When to use it:** Only when you genuinely have another offer, or a firm deadline from one.

### Step 1: Check it's real, then write it

~~~text
Act as my salary negotiation coach. I have a real offer from another company and I want to use it honestly.

Other offer: $[Amount] base, [Other key terms], deadline [Deadline]
My first choice: [Company]
Why it's my first choice: [Genuine reason]
Where things stand with my first choice: [They made an offer / I'm still interviewing]

If my first choice made an offer, write a message that tells them about the other offer as a question, not an ultimatum, and asks if there's room to get closer. If I'm still interviewing, write a message asking them to speed up so I can decide with both options in front of me.

Rules: don't name the other company unless I say so. Don't inflate the number. Compare total compensation, not just base, and tell me if the "better" offer is actually weaker once benefits and bonus are counted. If anything I've told you sounds like a bluff, stop and ask me before you write.
~~~

**Replace:** [Amount], [Other key terms], [Deadline], [Company], [Genuine reason], [They made an offer / I'm still interviewing]

## Respond to pushback

**What it does:** Gives you a calm reply to the common lines that end a negotiation too early.
**When to use it:** When you hear "This is our best offer", "This is our standard for this level", "We need to be fair to the team", "We'll review your salary after a year", or "Take it or leave it".

### Step 1: Write my reply

~~~text
Act as my salary negotiation coach. I countered and they said: "[What they said]"

My counter was: [My counter]
My three numbers: anchor $[Anchor], target $[Target], walk-away $[Walk-away]
What else matters to me, ranked: [Ranked list]

Write three possible replies, two or three sentences each:
1. The probe: ask if other parts of the package can move.
2. The reframe: name one or two specific beyond-base asks (or, for "we'll review after a year", a formal six-month review with a target, in the offer letter).
3. The pause: ask for a day to think it over.

Then tell me which reply fits best and why. Finish with one line reminding me to decide on my walk-away number and must-haves, not on pressure.
~~~

**Replace:** [What they said], [My counter], [Anchor], [Target], [Walk-away], [Ranked list]

## Ask for a raise or negotiate a promotion

**What it does:** Builds your case and your words for a raise or promotion at your current job.
**When to use it:** Before a raise conversation, a performance review, or when a promotion comes with a small increase.

### Step 1: Build the case

~~~text
Act as my career coach. I want to ask my manager for [a raise / more pay with my promotion].

My role now: [Job title], paid $[Current pay]
What's changed: [New responsibilities, bigger scope, new title]
Results from the past year: [Results, with numbers where I have them]
Market data for my role in [City] (real, with sources): [Market data]
The number I want: $[Number]
When budgets are set at my company: [Timing, if I know]

Do this:
1. Turn my results into three or four strong, specific bullet points. Use only what I've told you. If a result has no number, ask me whether I can measure it. Don't invent one.
2. Write a short email asking my manager for a 30-minute meeting.
3. Write a script for the conversation: my results, the market data, one clear number, then a pause, then "What would it take to make that happen?"
4. Write a reply for "not right now" that asks what would need to be true, and asks to agree on goals and a date in writing.
5. Write a two-sentence recap email to send after the meeting.
~~~

**Replace:** [a raise / more pay with my promotion], [Job title], [Current pay], [New responsibilities, bigger scope, new title], [Results], [City], [Market data], [Number], [Timing]

## Respond to a counter-offer from my current employer

**What it does:** Helps you think clearly about a counter-offer and gives you words to buy time or decline.
**When to use it:** After you resign and your employer offers more to keep you.

### Step 1: Think it through, then reply

~~~text
Act as my career coach. I resigned from [Current company] and my manager counter-offered: [The counter-offer]. My new offer from [New company] is: [The new offer].

Why I started looking: [Reasons]

Do this:
1. Write a one-line reply to buy time until [Day].
2. Ask me these questions one at a time and wait for each answer: Does the counter fix the reasons I started looking, or only the pay? Did it take a resignation to get it? Is it in writing, with dates? How would I feel telling the new company no?
3. Give me a plain summary of the trade-offs. Don't quote statistics about counter-offers. The decision is mine.
4. Then write what I need for my decision: a warm, firm decline of the counter-offer that moves the conversation to a smooth handoff, or (if I stay) a polite withdrawal email to the new company, plus a reminder to get every change in writing first.
~~~

**Replace:** [Current company], [The counter-offer], [New company], [The new offer], [Reasons], [Day]

## Calm the fear of a pulled offer

**What it does:** Gives you an honest view of the risk, a reassurance line, and words if an offer is withdrawn.
**When to use it:** When you're scared to ask, or an employer says the offer is off the table.

### Step 1: Check the risk and get the words

~~~text
Act as my salary negotiation coach. I'm worried that negotiating will make [Company] pull my offer.

What I'm planning to ask for: [My asks]
How the conversation has gone so far: [Notes]
Has the offer been withdrawn? [Yes or no]
Have I already accepted or resigned? [Yes or no]

Do this:
1. Review my asks and tell me honestly if any part sounds aggressive, like an ultimatum, or like a bluff. Suggest softer wording if so. Don't quote statistics.
2. Write a one-sentence reassurance line I can use mid-negotiation to show I'm excited and asking in good faith.
3. If the offer was withdrawn, write a short reply asking if the original offer is still possible (only if I'd take it).
4. If I've already accepted or resigned and the offer was pulled, tell me in one line to talk to an employment lawyer in my province or state, since my options depend on where I live and what I signed.
~~~

**Replace:** [Company], [My asks], [Notes], [Yes or no]
