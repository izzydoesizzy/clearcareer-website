# How to Negotiate Your Offer

**My plain-language guide to getting paid fairly, from the first recruiter call to the signed offer.**

> **This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice. Results vary.** Every employer, role and market is different, and no script guarantees an outcome. Use these as starting points and adapt them to your situation.

**Time:** 25 minutes to read. Come back to the section that matches where you are.
**What you'll need:** Your three numbers (anchor, target, walk-away), the job posting, and any offer details you have so far.

---

Here's the thing about negotiating: it feels scary because it feels like a fight. It isn't.

By the time you get an offer, they've picked you. They've spent weeks on interviews. They want this to work. You want this to work. Now the two of you are figuring out the terms. That's it.

This guide is the "why" and the "when". Guides 2 to 5 give you the exact words. Guide 6 is the worksheet you'll use to compare offers. Read this one first, then keep the scripts open when the moment comes.

I'd rather you ask and hear "no" than never ask at all. A polite, specific ask is a normal part of hiring. Recruiters handle them all the time.

## Start With Your Three Numbers

Before any salary conversation, you need three numbers written down:

| Number | What it is | How to set it (short version) |
|---|---|---|
| **Walk-away** | The lowest you'll accept | Your real monthly costs, times 12, plus a buffer. Those costs come out of take-home pay, so the salary you need before tax is higher |
| **Target** | What you'd be genuinely happy with | Around the middle of the market range you found across several sources |
| **Anchor** | Your opening number when you have to go first | The top of a realistic range for your skills and location, often 10 to 15% above your target |

The **Salary Number Generator** walks you through all three step by step, with a budget worksheet and four salary sources. It pairs well with this kit. If you already have your numbers, you're ready.

Put them on a sticky note. Keep that note in front of you on every call.

## My Principles

### 1. Be grateful and specific

Thank them, and mean it. Then name something specific you're excited about: the team, the project, the problem you'll get to solve.

Gratitude and a counter can live in the same message. "Thank you, I'm really excited about this, and I'd like to talk about the base salary" is a perfectly normal sentence.

### 2. Don't give the first number when you can avoid it

When you can, let them share their range first. If the posting lists a range, start there.

If you have to go first, give your **anchor**, or a range that starts at your **target**. Never your walk-away. The bottom of any range you say out loud is the number they'll remember.

And you don't owe anyone your current salary. Some provinces and states limit whether employers can even ask. (Guide 2 has the scripts for all of this.)

### 3. Negotiate the package, not just the base

Base salary matters most because so much is built on it: future raises, and often your bonus and retirement match too. Start there.

But it's not the only lever. Signing bonus, start date, title, vacation, remote work, learning budget and equity are all fair game. (More on this below, and in Guide 4.)

### 4. Put every ask in one conversation

Decide your one to three asks before you counter. Rank them. Present them together.

Asking for one thing, getting it, then coming back for another wears down goodwill fast. One clean ask is easier for them to take to whoever approves it.

### 5. Get it in writing

A verbal offer isn't an offer yet. Ask for the written version before you respond.

And when you agree on new terms, get a revised offer letter that shows them. Promises like "we'll revisit your salary in six months" only count if they're on paper.

### 6. Silence is fine

After you say your number, stop talking. Count to ten in your head if you need to.

Silence feels awful in the moment. It's not your job to fill it. Let them respond. Don't rush in with "but I'm flexible" or "if that's too much, I understand."

### 7. Be willing to walk

Your walk-away number only works if you mean it. Decide it before the call, not during.

Walking away is about math, not pride. If the final offer is below your walk-away and your must-haves aren't met, a polite "no, thank you" is a complete answer. It helps to know your best alternative too: another offer, staying where you are, or the runway to keep searching.

### 8. Never bluff

Only mention another offer if it's real. Don't invent a deadline. Don't make up a number.

If a bluff gets called, you lose trust you can't win back, and sometimes the offer with it.

### Stage 1: Before the first call

Set your three numbers. Check the posting for a range. Write down your must-haves (the things you need, like remote work or a minimum vacation) and your deal-breakers (the things that end it).

Two minutes of prep here saves you from making up a number on the spot later.

### Stage 2: The recruiter screen

The salary question usually shows up early. That's fair: the recruiter doesn't want to waste your time or theirs.

Your goal is to learn their range and confirm you're in the same ballpark, without locking yourself into a number. Ask about the rest of the package and the hiring timeline while you're there.

### Stage 3: The interviews

This isn't the time to negotiate. It's the time to build your case.

Every story you tell about a real result is a reason to pay you more later. Take notes on what they care about most. Those notes become your talking points when you counter.

### Stage 4: The verbal offer

Thank them. Show you're excited. Ask for the offer in writing. Agree on when you'll get back to them (24 to 72 hours is a normal ask).

Don't say yes or no on that call. Even if the offer is great.

### Stage 5: Review

Fill in the worksheet in Guide 6. Look at total compensation, not just base. Compare the offer to your three numbers and your must-haves. Then pick your asks and rank them.

### Stage 6: The counter

One message, by phone or email, with every ask in it and one clear number for each.

Phone or email? Both work. A call feels warmer and you hear their reaction. Email lets you get the words right and gives you a record. A common approach is to call, then send a short email that recaps what you discussed. Pick the one where you'll feel calmer.

### Stage 7: Their response

They may need a few days to get approvals. That's normal. If you haven't heard back after three business days, send one friendly check-in.

They'll usually come back with a yes, a partial yes, or a firm no on base. If base is firm, move to the beyond-base asks in Guide 4. If you get pushback, Guide 5 has the replies.

### Stage 8: Decide

Is the final offer at or above your walk-away? Are your must-haves met? Any deal-breakers? If it works, accept in writing and confirm every term. If it doesn't, you can make one last ask or decline gracefully.

### Stage 9: Sign

Read the final offer letter line by line. Check the start date, title, base, bonus terms, equity terms, vacation, remote arrangement, probation period, when benefits start, and any restrictive clauses (non-compete, non-solicit, intellectual property).

Don't resign from your current job until you have the signed offer back and any conditions (background check, references) are cleared.

## The Most Common Mistakes

1. **Saying yes on the spot.** Even a great offer deserves a day. "I'm excited. Can I review the details and get back to you by Thursday?"
2. **Opening with your walk-away number.** You just set your ceiling at your floor.
3. **Saying "I'm flexible" with no number.** To the other side, that sounds like "I'll take whatever you offer."
4. **Sharing your current salary when you don't have to.** What you make now isn't what this role is worth.
5. **Asking for one thing at a time.** Put it all in one ask.
6. **Apologizing and over-explaining.** "Sorry, I hate to ask, I know budgets are tight..." Say your number. Stop.
7. **Making it about your bills.** Your rent is real, but it's not their reason to pay more. Talk about the role, your results and the market.
8. **Bluffing about other offers.** Ever.
9. **Looking only at base.** Or the reverse: giving up base for perks that are worth less than they sound.
10. **Trusting verbal promises.** If it matters, it goes in the offer letter.
11. **Resigning before the offer is signed.** Get the signed letter first.
12. **Not practising out loud.** Reading a script in your head is not the same as saying it. Say it three times. Role-play it with the Claude skill or a friend.

## What's Negotiable Beyond Base

| Item | What to ask for | Good to know |
|---|---|---|
| **Signing bonus** | A one-time payment to close the gap when base is capped | Check the clawback: many have to be repaid if you leave within a set time |
| **Start date** | More time between jobs, or time to finish commitments | Costs them little, which makes it one of the easier asks |
| **Title** | A title that matches the scope (Senior, Lead, Manager) | Affects how you're seen now and what you can apply for next |
| **Vacation** | Match what you have now, or one extra week | Some companies have fixed policies. Matching your current amount is the strongest case |
| **Remote or hybrid** | A set number of remote days, written into the offer | "We're flexible" in an interview is not the same as a written arrangement |
| **Learning budget** | Courses, certifications or conferences tied to the role | Easier to approve when you name the exact course or event |
| **Equity** | A larger grant, a clearer vesting schedule | Private company equity is hard to value. Ask an accountant about tax |
| **Early salary review** | A formal review at six months with a set target | Only worth it in writing, with clear goals |
| **Bonus** | A higher target, or a guaranteed first-year bonus | Especially if you're giving up a bonus by leaving your current job |
| **Relocation** | Moving costs, temporary housing | Check if it has a repayment clause too |
| **Benefits start date** | Coverage from day one instead of after a waiting period | Matters most if you'll have a gap in coverage |

Rank these by what matters to you. Lead with your top one or two. If you can only win one thing, make it the one that will still matter in two years.

## Canada and the US: What's Different

- **Pay transparency:** Several provinces (including British Columbia, Prince Edward Island and Ontario) and a growing list of US states (including California, Colorado, New York and Washington) require pay ranges in many job postings. The rules, thresholds and start dates differ, so check yours. When a range is posted, it's your starting point.
- **Pay history:** Some provinces and states limit or ban employers from asking about your current or past pay. Either way, you don't have to share it.
- **Vacation:** In Canada, each province sets a legal minimum (two weeks to start in most provinces, rising with years of service). The US has no federal minimum, so every vacation day is up to the offer.
- **Health and retirement:** In the US, employer health insurance can be a big part of an offer's value, so compare premiums and deductibles closely. In Canada, compare extended health and dental coverage, plus any RRSP match or pension.
- **Restrictive clauses:** Rules on non-competes and similar clauses differ a lot by province and state. If one worries you, get a quick review from an employment lawyer before you sign.
