# How to Read Your Career Clarity Audit

**Five titles, five searches, three numbers. Here's what each one means and what to do with it this week.**

Your audit is a starting point, not a verdict. I built it to answer two questions fast: which jobs fit what you've already done, and what you should be asking to get paid. This guide walks through every part of it so you can put it to work today.

**Time:** About 20 minutes to read, and 10 minutes a day this week to act on it. **What you'll need:** Your audit, your resume and your LinkedIn profile.

*This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice. Results vary.*

---

## What Your Fit % Means

Your fit score is a rough read of how much of a job your resume already shows you can do.

Here's how it's worked out. For each title, Claude lists the 6 to 10 things job posts for that title usually ask for. Then it checks your resume for each one. Clear evidence gets full credit. Thin evidence gets half. The total becomes a percentage, rounded to the nearest 5. Every title in your audit scored 70% or higher, or it didn't make the list.

| Fit | What it tells you | What I'd do |
|-----|-------------------|-------------|
| **90% or more** | You've basically done this job already. | Apply with confidence. |
| **80% to 89%** | Most of it is there, with one or two gaps. | Cover the gaps in your resume or cover letter, then apply. |
| **70% to 79%** | Real overlap, with a gap to close. | Talk to people in the role before you apply cold. |

Two things your fit score is not:

- **It's not your chance of getting hired.** Hiring depends on timing, the team, the other people applying and a hundred things no score can see.
- **It's not an ATS score.** (ATS means applicant tracking system: the software many employers use to sort applications.) Your audit doesn't run your resume through one.

Here's the part most people miss. **Your fit score is only as good as your resume.** If you've done something that isn't on your resume, the score can't see it. When a score feels low, read the "Gap to close" line. A lot of the time, the experience is there and your resume just doesn't say it. Add it, honestly, and the gap closes.

## The Four Kinds of Titles

Your 5 titles come in a mix on purpose. Searching for one title is like fishing with one hook.

- **Direct (2 titles):** the job you do now, under the names employers use. These are your bread and butter. Apply now.
- **Adjacent (1 title):** a neighbouring role that uses most of your skills in a different function or industry. Your story matters most here: tell them why your skills carry over.
- **Emerging (1 title):** a newer or growing title that fits your skills. Companies name these jobs differently, so try a few versions of the title in your search.
- **Stretch (1 title):** a level up, or a bigger scope. Apply to a few, and reach out to people who already have the job.

**Demand** (High, Medium or Low) is a read, not a statistic. Check it yourself: click the LinkedIn link for each title and look at how many results come up. Compare your 5 titles side by side. That tells you where the volume is right now.

## How to Use Your Titles in Your Search

1. **Search every title, not just the one you have now.** Paste the keywords from your audit into LinkedIn, Indeed, Job Bank (in Canada) and the careers pages of companies you like.
2. **Match the top of your resume to the job.** When you apply for a Direct title, use that title in your resume summary. When you apply for an Adjacent title, lead your summary with the skills that carry over.
3. **Keep your past job titles true.** Never change a title you actually held. If your old title was unusual, you can add the common name in brackets after it, as long as it honestly describes the job. For example: "Client Happiness Lead (Customer Success)".
4. **Read 3 posts for each title before you apply.** Highlight the words that keep coming up. If you've done those things, make sure your resume uses those words.

## How to Use Your Titles on LinkedIn

1. **Open each search link from your audit.** Check the location, then set the "Date posted" filter to the past week so you see fresh roles first.
2. **Turn on a job alert** for your top 2 or 3 searches. LinkedIn will send you new matches, so you stop doom scrolling and start responding.
3. **Put your top Direct title in your LinkedIn headline,** plus one or two keywords from your Adjacent title. Recruiters search by title. Your headline is your billboard.
4. **Add your titles to your Open to Work job preferences.** You can choose whether only recruiters see it or everyone does.
5. **Find people who have these jobs.** Search a title, filter by People, and look for folks at companies you like. These are your first networking conversations.

## How to Use Your Three Numbers

Your salary section shows a percentile table. Here's what that means in plain words:

- **25th percentile:** a quarter of people in this role earn less than this.
- **50th (the median):** the middle. Half earn more, half earn less.
- **75th:** a quarter earn more than this.
- **90th:** only about 1 in 10 earn more than this.

Your three numbers come from that table:

| Number | Where it sits | When you use it |
|--------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Anchor** | Around the 85th to 90th percentile | Your opening number if they make you name one first |
| **Target** | The 65th to 75th percentile | The number you're working toward |
| **Walk-away** | The 25th percentile | Your floor. Below this, you decline or keep looking |

**An example, with made-up numbers.** Sam is a fictional logistics coordinator. Say Sam's table reads $52K at the 25th, $58K at the 50th, $65K at the 75th and $72K at the 90th. Sam's anchor lands around $70K, the target around $62K to $65K, and the walk-away at $52K. Those numbers are invented just to show the maths. Yours come from your sources.

**How to say it.** When a recruiter asks early, ask for their range first:

> "I'd love to learn more about the role first. What range do you have budgeted for it?"

If they push for a number, give a range from your target up to your anchor:

> "Based on my research for this role in [City], I'm looking at [Target] to [Anchor]."

Then stop talking. Let them respond. Your walk-away stays private. You never say it out loud.

**Three more things before you use your numbers:**

- **Your walk-away can be higher than the 25th percentile.** If the lowest pay you can live on is higher than that row, your walk-away is your number, not the table's.
- **These are base pay numbers.** Bonus, benefits, vacation and retirement matching (an RRSP match or pension in Canada, a 401(k) match in the US) all count when you compare offers.
- **Check every number.** Click each source in your audit. Look at the dates, because government data often runs a year or more behind. Anything marked "(estimate, confirm)" is a placeholder until you've checked it against real job posts. Several provinces and US states now require pay ranges in job posts, and those posted ranges are some of the best data you can get. Rules change, so check the current ones where you live.

## What to Do This Week

Pick one of these a day. Each one takes about 10 minutes.

1. **Turn on your alerts.** Open all 5 LinkedIn links and turn on job alerts for your top 2 or 3.
2. **Update your headline.** Put your top Direct title in your LinkedIn headline, with one or two skills that back it up.
3. **Close one gap.** Pick your top title, read 3 posts for it, and add one thing you've really done that your resume doesn't show yet.
4. **Check your numbers.** Find 3 job posts for your top title that list a pay range. Write them down next to your table. Do they line up?
5. **Talk to one person.** Find someone who has one of your 5 titles and send this:

> "Hi [First name], I'm exploring [Job title] roles and saw you've been doing this at [Company]. Would you be open to a 15-minute chat about what the job is really like? No pressure at all."

6. **Practise your answer.** Write your anchor, target and walk-away on a sticky note. Say the two lines above out loud three times.

Clarity first, then volume. Once you know which jobs to go after and what to ask for, every application, message and interview gets easier. Results vary, but you'll be working from a plan instead of a guess.
