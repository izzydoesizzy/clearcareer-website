# The Cold Outreach Campaign

**Find the one person who hires for the role, find one real thing they've done, and send a short email that could only have been written to them.**

Applying online and waiting is the default. And it's lonely. You hit submit, your resume joins a pile, and then you refresh your inbox for three weeks.

Here's what I want you to do instead. Pick your best-fit companies. At each one, find the person who would actually be your boss. Find one specific thing they've done: a post, a talk, a project. Then write them a short note, 75 to 125 words, about their work and a bit of yours, and ask for 15 minutes.

That's a cold outreach campaign. It's the same method I use to build outreach plans: one company, one person, one email, one follow-up. This guide walks you through it end to end, with the email rules, real examples (with fictional people) and a tracker.

The first email feels bold. By the fifth, it feels normal.

**Time:** About 30 minutes per company the first time, faster after that. **What you'll need:** Your resume (or 2 to 3 wins with numbers), your ranked company list (from Guide 2), your email and a spreadsheet.

---

## The campaign in 7 steps

1. Pick your companies
2. Find one person at each
3. Work out their email address
4. Find your hook
5. Write the email
6. Follow up
7. Track it

---

## Step 1: Pick your companies

Start with 10. Take them from the top of your company scorecard if you have one. Look for:

- **Your strongest fit.** The companies where your skills and experience line up best.
- **A mix of sizes.** Some large, some mid-size, some growing startups. They respond differently, and you want to learn what works for you.
- **A reason to write.** A team that's growing, a leader who posts or speaks, a recent launch. These make warm notes easy.
- **Different angles.** Ten companies that all get the same email is a mail merge. Ten companies with ten different reasons is a campaign.

For each one, write one sentence: why this company is worth an email.

---

## Step 2: Find one person at each company

You're looking for one person. In this order:

1. The hiring manager or team lead for your kind of role
2. The head of that department
3. A talent acquisition or recruiting lead
4. Another relevant senior person in that part of the company

Who that is depends on the size of the company:

- **Under about 50 people:** the founder, the CEO or the head of the function
- **Mid-size:** the director or manager who leads the team
- **Large:** the manager of the specific team. A recruiter is your second contact, not your first.

Where to look:

- **The job post.** Some LinkedIn postings show "Meet the hiring team". Many descriptions say "reporting to the [Title]". Search for that title.
- **LinkedIn.** Open the company page, click People, and type a keyword for the function plus a level ("operations director", "head of design").
- **The company website.** Team, About or Leadership pages, and blog author pages.
- **Google.** Paste this in, with your details:

~~~text
site:linkedin.com/in "[Company]" "[Title]"
~~~

- **Press releases, conference speaker pages and podcast guest pages.**

Two checks before you use a name:

- **Do they still hold that role?** A press release from three years ago isn't enough. Look for something recent.
- **Is it definitely them?** If two people share a name, use the profile and the company to tell them apart.

Only use people who are publicly listed, and never guess. A wrong name is worse than no email at all.

Can't tell who the hiring manager is? Pick the closest person and ask them to point you the right way. People forward emails to the right colleague all the time.

---

## Step 3: Work out their email address

Work through these in order. Stop as soon as you have a solid address.

1. **Look for a published address.** Their LinkedIn contact info, their posts, a personal website, a speaker bio, a podcast page, or a press release that lists them as a media contact.
2. **Work out the company's pattern.** Find any one real, published email at the same company: a press release, a job post that lists a recruiter's email, a blog author page. That shows you the format. Apply it to your person's name. The common patterns:
   - first.last@company.com
   - first@company.com
   - firstlast@company.com
   - flast@company.com (first initial, then last name)
   - firstl@company.com (first name, then last initial)
   - first_last@company.com
3. **Use an email finder or verifier.** Plenty have a free tier with a few searches a month. You don't need to pay for anything to run this method.
4. **Try the free checks.** Google the address in quotes. Or start a new email in Gmail, type the address in the To field and hover over it. If a name or photo pops up, it's probably real. (No pop-up doesn't prove it's wrong.)

How to read what you find:

- **"Valid" or "verified":** send it.
- **"Accept-all" or "risky":** the company's server accepts everything, so the tool can't tell. Send to your best guess. If it bounces, try the next pattern.
- **It bounced:** try the next most likely pattern, or reach them on LinkedIn.

Until you've checked it, treat your guess as a guess. In your tracker, write "pattern guess" next to it.

One person, one best guess, one email. Don't send the same email to five guessed addresses at once.

---

## Step 4: Find your hook

The hook is one specific, real thing the person has done. It's the first line of your email, and it's the reason they keep reading.

What counts:

- A post or article they wrote
- A talk, panel or podcast they were on
- A quote in a news story
- A project, launch or initiative they led
- A promotion, or a move into a new role
- A milestone for their team

What doesn't count: their job title, a description of the company, or praise like "your impressive career".

Where to look: their recent LinkedIn posts, the company blog and newsroom, conference agendas, podcast listings and industry news.

Three rules:

1. **Keep the link.** Save it in your tracker. Read it properly before you write.
2. **Pick something recent.** The last 12 months is ideal.
3. **Can't find anything about the person?** Use something recent about the company (a launch, a new office) and say so honestly.

## Step 5: Write the email

### The shape

75 to 125 words, not counting your sign-off. Short is respectful. It says "I know you're busy."

1. **Greeting:** "Hi [First name]," then a blank line.
2. **Opening (1 to 2 sentences):** about them, using your hook. One specific detail that proves you actually read it, watched it or listened.
3. **Bridge (2 to 3 sentences):** connect their work to one of your wins. State it plainly. It should feel like "oh, I've worked on something like that", not a list of achievements.
4. **Transition and ask (2 to 3 sentences):** why you want to talk, then ask for 15 minutes, then give them an easy out.
5. **Sign-off:** your name, plus your email or LinkedIn.

Write it the way you talk. Your words, your tone. Read it out loud before you send it. If it sounds like a press release, start again.

### Pick an opener

Eight patterns. Use a different one for each email, so no two read the same. (All the examples are fictional.)

1. **"I loved [their specific thing], especially [one detail]."**
   "I loved your panel on cutting empty truck kilometres, especially the point that backhaul planning starts when the quote goes out."
2. **"Your [thing] got me thinking about [related idea from your own work]."**
   "Your post on onboarding checklists got me thinking about the 30-day plan I built for new pharmacy techs."
3. **"I've been following [Company]'s work on [topic], and [specific observation]."**
   "I've been following Lumen Health's work on virtual care, and the move to evening appointments looks like a smart fit for shift workers."
4. **"[Specific detail from their work] really stuck with me because [personal reason]."**
   "Your line about fixing the handoff, not the hire, really stuck with me because handoffs were most of my last project."
5. **"I was reading about [their project] and [curious question or observation]."**
   "I was reading about Northwind Bank's new small business portal and wondered how your team picked the first features to launch."
6. **"Something you said about [topic] in [place] resonated, [brief why]."**
   "Something you said about vendor scorecards on a supply chain podcast in March resonated, since I rebuilt ours last year."
7. **"Your path from [A] to [B] is the kind of move I find really interesting, [why]."**
   "Your path from nursing to health tech product work is the kind of move I find really interesting, since I'm making a similar one from teaching."
8. **"I noticed [Company] just [recent thing], [your genuine reaction]."**
   "I noticed Bluepeak Software just opened a Halifax office, and having helped open a regional office before, I know how busy that first year gets."

Notice what they have in common: curiosity, not flattery. You're starting a conversation, not leaving a compliment.

### Pick a transition

Five ways to say why you want to talk. Vary them too.

1. "I'd love to hear more about how your team is approaching [specific thing]."
2. "I have a couple of questions about [specific part of their work or the company's direction]."
3. "I'm curious how [something from their work] is playing out now."
4. "I'd love to learn more about [specific initiative or product] and where it's headed."
5. "I keep thinking about [specific detail from their work] and would love to hear your perspective."

### The ask and the exit clause

One small ask: 15 minutes.

- "Would you be open to a 15-minute call in the next couple of weeks?"
- "Could I borrow 15 minutes of your time next week or the week after?"

Then an exit clause, so saying no feels easy. It takes the pressure off both of you:

- "No pressure at all if the timing doesn't work."
- "Totally understand if you're swamped."
- "If now isn't a good time, no worries at all."
- "If you're not the right person for this, I'd be grateful for a pointer."

### The subject line

2 to 5 words. Think text to a friend, not a business memo.

- "Your logistics post + a question"
- "Fellow alumni, quick question"
- "[Company] + [your target role]?"
- "Your panel on backhauls"
- "Question about the Halifax office"

Never "Re:" or "Fwd:" unless it really is a reply.

### Never write these

- "I'm reaching out..." or "I wanted to reach out..."
- "I hope this email finds you well" or "To whom it may concern"
- "I'm confident I'd be a great fit" (let them decide that)
- "Synergy", "leverage", "value-add", "results-driven", "detail-oriented"
- "Genuinely impressive", "incredible", "amazing", "brilliant", "inspiring"
- "Stopped me mid-scroll", "caught my eye", "jumped out at me", "I stumbled across", "I couldn't help but notice", "I was blown away"
- "Pick your brain"
- "Thanks in advance" or "Looking forward to hearing from you"
- An attachment. Offer your resume instead.

### Before and after (fictional)

Here's the email most people send:

~~~text
Subject: Experienced operations professional seeking new opportunities

Hi Morgan,

I hope this email finds you well. I'm reaching out because I'm a results-driven operations professional with over 8 years of experience, and I'm confident I'd be a great fit for your team. Your career is genuinely impressive. I'd love to pick your brain about any openings you might have. I've attached my resume for your review.

Thanks in advance!
Alex
~~~

What's wrong with it: the subject line is a job ad. It's all about Alex. Nothing in it shows Alex knows who Morgan is. "Results-driven" and "genuinely impressive" mean nothing. There's an attachment from a stranger. And "any openings" is a big, vague ask.

Here's the same email, rebuilt:

~~~text
Subject: Your panel on empty kilometres

Hi Morgan,

I loved your panel on cutting empty truck kilometres, especially the point that backhaul planning starts when the quote goes out.

That matches what I saw at a regional distributor. I rebuilt our route planning sheet with the dispatch team, and late deliveries fell from 14% to 6% over two quarters.

I'd love to hear more about how your team is approaching backhauls as the Ontario network grows. Would you be open to a 15-minute call in the next couple of weeks? No pressure at all if the timing doesn't work.

Alex Chen
alex.chen@email.example
~~~

93 words. It starts with Morgan. It proves Alex paid attention. One win, with a real number. One small ask. One easy out.

### The checklist before you hit send

- [ ] Every fact about them matches the source, and I've read the source
- [ ] Every win and number is mine and true
- [ ] 75 to 125 words, not counting my sign-off
- [ ] Subject line is 2 to 5 words
- [ ] One ask, one exit clause
- [ ] None of the "never write these" phrases
- [ ] I read it out loud, and it sounds like me
- [ ] I checked the spelling of their name and the company
- [ ] If there's a posted job, I applied through the careers page too

## Step 6: Follow up

Busy people miss emails. A follow-up isn't pushy. It's normal.

- **When:** 5 to 7 business days after your first email, if there's no reply.
- **Where:** in the same thread, so they can see your first note.
- **How long:** 2 to 3 sentences.
- **What:** bring your email back to the top of their inbox. Add one small new thing if you have one (a thought, a relevant link, a quick update).
- **Never:** "Did you get my email?" or anything with guilt in it.

Then one final note about a week later, at most. After two follow-ups with no reply, stop and move on. You can try a second person at the same company.

### Follow-up 1

~~~text
Hi [First name],

Bringing this back to the top of your inbox in case it got buried. I'd still love 15 minutes on [the topic from your first email], whenever it suits you. If not, no worries at all.

[Your name]
~~~

### The final note

~~~text
Hi [First name],

I'll leave it here so I don't crowd your inbox. If your team ever wants to compare notes on [topic], I'd be glad to talk. Thanks again for [their post, talk or project], it's stayed with me.

[Your name]
~~~

And when someone does reply? Answer within a business day, offer two or three times, and keep it short.

## Your sending plan

- **Start with your top 3 to 5.**
- **Send 2 or 3 a day,** not all ten at once. Each one stays personal, and you have time to reply to anyone who answers.
- **Before each send:** check the address, reread the hook's source, fill every [bracket].
- **Follow up** after 5 to 7 business days. Final note about a week later. Then stop.
- **Block 15 minutes a day** to update your tracker and send whatever's due.

A simple week:

| Day | What to do |
|---|---|
| Monday | Pick 10 companies. Find one person at each. |
| Tuesday | Work out email addresses. Find a hook for each. |
| Wednesday | Write and send your first 3. |
| Thursday | Send the next 3. Reply to anyone who wrote back. |
| Friday | Send the last 4. Put every follow-up date in your calendar. |

---

## The rules of the road

- **One person at a time.** These are personal, one-to-one emails, not marketing. Use your real name and contact details, never add anyone to a list, and if someone asks you to stop, stop. That follows the spirit of Canada's anti-spam law (CASL) and the US CAN-SPAM Act. This is general guidance, not legal advice.
- **Keep your facts true.** Never claim a connection you don't have, and never stretch a number. People check.
- **Results vary.** Some people reply the same day. Some never do, and it's almost never about you. Keep sending.
