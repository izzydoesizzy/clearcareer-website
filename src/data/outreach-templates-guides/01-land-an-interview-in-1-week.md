# Land an Interview in 1 Week

**My method for skipping the application pile: find the person who hires for the role, find their email, send a short note, and follow up.**

Applying online and waiting is the default. You hit submit, your resume joins a pile of hundreds, and then nothing happens. Maybe an automated "we regret to inform you" shows up a month later.

I don't want that for you. So let's flip it.

Instead of waiting for the hiring manager to find you, you find them. You look up the person who would be your boss, work out their email address, and send a short, specific note about the work they need done. Then you follow up, because busy people miss emails.

The first one feels bold. By the tenth, it feels normal. This guide gives you the whole method, a day-by-day plan for your first week, and five templates to get you started.

One honest note: the title is the goal, not a promise. Some people get a reply in two days. Some need a few weeks of steady sending. Results vary. But after one week of doing this, you'll know far more about your market than you would after a month of waiting.

**Time:** About an hour a day for 7 days. **What you'll need:** A LinkedIn account (it doesn't need to be polished yet), your resume, a spreadsheet, and your email.

---

## The method in 6 steps

### Step 1: Pick your targets

Start with 10 companies you'd actually want to work for. Mix them up:

- Some with a posted job that fits you
- Some with no posting, where you know the kind of team you'd join

A posted job tells you a team has a need right now. A company with no posting can still have a need it hasn't advertised yet. Both are fair game.

For each company, write down the role (or the team) you're aiming for. That tells you who to look for next.

### Step 2: Find the person who hires for the role

You're looking for the person you'd report to. That's usually one or two levels above the role.

- **Small company (under about 50 people):** the founder, the CEO, or the head of the function
- **Mid-size company:** the director or manager who leads the team
- **Large company:** the manager of the specific team. A recruiter is a good second contact, not your first.

Here's how to find them on LinkedIn:

1. Search the company name and open the company page (not a group or a showcase page).
2. Click **People** (or the "employees" link near the top of the page).
3. Type a keyword for the function plus a level: "marketing director", "head of finance", "engineering manager".
4. Add a location filter if the role is local.
5. Open the profiles. Look for the person whose team matches the job.

More places to look:

- **The job post.** LinkedIn posts sometimes show "Meet the hiring team" with a name. Many descriptions also say "reporting to the [Title]". Search for that title.
- **The company website.** Check the Team, About or Leadership page.
- **Google.** Try `site:linkedin.com/in "[Company]" "[Title]"` to search LinkedIn profiles through Google.

Pick two people per company: the likely hiring manager and a backup (a team lead, or someone already doing the job). Save their name, title and profile link in your tracker.

Can't tell who the hiring manager is? Pick the closest person and ask them to point you the right way (Template 4 below). People forward emails to the right colleague all the time.

### Step 3: Find their email address

Work through these in order. Stop as soon as you have a solid address.

1. **Look for a published address.** Check their LinkedIn "Contact info", their posts, a personal website, a conference speaker bio, a podcast page, or a press release with a media contact.
2. **Work out the company's email pattern.** Find any one real email at the company: a press release, a job post that lists a recruiter's email, a blog author page. That shows you the format. Then apply it to your person's name. The common patterns:
   - first.last@company.com
   - first@company.com
   - firstlast@company.com
   - flast@company.com (first initial, then last name)
   - firstl@company.com (first name, then last initial)
   - first_last@company.com
3. **Use an email finder or verifier.** There are plenty of these, and many have a free tier with a small number of searches each month. You enter the company's website and the person's name, and the tool suggests the likely address and how confident it is. A verifier checks whether the company's mail server will accept an address. You don't need to pay for anything to use this method.
4. **Try the free checks.** Google the address in quotes. Or start a new email in Gmail, type the address in the To field and hover over it. If a name or photo pops up, the address is probably real. (No pop-up doesn't prove it's wrong.)

How to read what you find:

- **"Valid" or "verified":** send it.
- **"Accept-all" or "risky":** the company's server accepts everything, so the tool can't tell. Send to your best guess. If it bounces, try the next pattern.
- **It bounced:** try the next most likely pattern, or reach them on LinkedIn instead.

Don't send the same email to five guessed addresses at once. One person, one best guess, one email.

### Step 4: Write a short, specific email

There are two ways in:

1. **Ask for advice.** You ask for 15 minutes of their perspective on the team, the role or how to break in. This is low pressure, people like helping, and it often leads to introductions. It's my favourite starting point, especially if you're changing careers or there's no posting.
2. **Say you're interested in a role.** You point to a posted job, show you fit its top priority, and ask for a short call. This works best when there's a posting and you're a strong match.

Either way, follow these rules:

- **Keep it under 120 words.** Short, specific emails get more replies than long ones.
- **Open with them.** Something specific and true: their post, their team's launch, the top priority in the job post.
- **Prove it in one or two lines.** A win from your work, with a number if you have one.
- **Ask for one small thing.** A 15-minute call. Not "a job". Not "any opportunities".
- **Keep the subject line short and plain.** No clickbait, and never fake a "Re:".
- **Skip attachments.** Offer your resume instead. Plain text, no fancy formatting.

### Step 5: Send it the right way

- Send from your personal email, with your full name. Add a simple signature: name, phone, LinkedIn URL.
- I like Tuesday to Thursday mornings in their time zone. It's not magic. It just avoids the Monday pile-up and the Friday checkout.
- Send 5 to 10 a day at most, each one written for that person. This isn't a mass mailing.
- Log each email in your tracker the minute you send it, with a follow-up date.

### Step 6: Follow up and track

- **Follow-up 1:** 4 to 6 business days later. Reply in the same thread. Two or three sentences.
- **Follow-up 2:** about a week after that. Add something useful, or close the loop politely.
- **Then stop.** Three touches in total. If you still want in, try a different person at the same company.

The Follow-Ups guide has the full cadence and more templates.

Your tracker can be a simple spreadsheet with these columns:

| Company | Person | Title | Email | How I found it | Sent | Follow-up 1 | Follow-up 2 | Reply? | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Some email add-ons can tell you when an email was opened. Treat that as a hint, not proof. Some email apps open messages automatically for privacy reasons, and some block tracking completely. If an email bounces or gets nothing after both follow-ups, the person may have left, changed roles or gone on leave. Find someone else on the team and start again.

## A quick note on the rules

These are one-to-one personal emails, not marketing. Canada's anti-spam law (CASL) and the US CAN-SPAM Act are mainly about commercial and bulk email, but follow their spirit anyway: write to one person at a time about their work, use your real name and contact details, never add anyone to a mailing list, and if someone asks you to stop, stop. This is general guidance, not legal advice.

## "But what if..."

Feeling nervous right now? That's totally normal. Almost everyone feels it before their first send. Here are the worries I hear most.

**Worry: It feels weird to email someone I've never met.**
It does, the first few times. Then it gets easier. You're not bothering them. You're a qualified person reaching out about work they need done.

**Worry: Why would they want to help me?**
Lots of people like helping, especially when the ask is small and specific. Say something kind and true about their work. And remember: a hiring manager with an open role wants good people to find them.

**Worry: What if they ask how I got their email?**
I've walked a lot of people through this, and it rarely comes up. If it does, be honest (Template 5). Working out someone's email shows initiative.

**Worry: What if they ignore me?**
Silence isn't rejection. People are busy, and emails get buried. Follow up twice, then move on to the next person. If you get a "no", thank them and ask if someone else would be better to talk to.

**Worry: Am I going around the recruiter?**
If there's a posting, apply through the careers page too, and say so in your email. Then you're not going around anyone. You're making sure a real person sees your name.

## Your first week, day by day

**Day 1: Pick your 10.** Choose 10 target companies and the role or team at each. Set up your tracker.

**Day 2: Find the people.** Find two people per company on LinkedIn: the likely hiring manager and a backup. That's 20 names.

**Day 3: Find the emails and the hooks.** Work out email addresses for your top 10 people. For each one, note a specific, true thing to open with: a post, a launch, the top line of the job post.

**Day 4: Send your first 5.** Write and send five emails. If there's a posting, apply through the careers page first.

**Day 5: Send the next 5.** Then send LinkedIn connection requests to the same people, with a short note and no pitch.

**Day 6: Answer and prepare.** Reply within a day to anyone who wrote back. Book the calls. Get ready: a 30-second intro, two wins, and three questions for them.

**Day 7: Review and reload.** Put follow-up dates in your calendar (4 to 6 business days after each send). Pick your next 10 companies. Then rest. You earned it.

Next week? Lather, rinse, repeat. Results vary, and some weeks will be quiet. Keep showing up anyway.

## Templates for your first week

### Template 1: The Advice Ask

**Subject:** Your [post or talk] on [topic]

~~~text
Hi [First name],

I came across your [post, talk or podcast episode] on [topic], and [the specific point] stuck with me. It lines up with how I approached [related thing you did].

A bit about me: I'm a [Your title] in [industry]. Most recently, I [win with a number]. I'd like to do more [type of work] at a company like [Company].

Would you be open to a 15-minute call [next Tuesday or Wednesday]? I'd love your advice on how to break into [role or team].

Thanks,
[Your name]
[LinkedIn URL]
~~~

**When to use it:** You're exploring a field or a company, there's no posting yet, or you're changing careers and want an insider's view.

**Make it yours:** The opener only works if you actually read or watched the thing. Quote one idea in your own words. Name two specific days for the call. It's easier to say yes to "Tuesday or Wednesday" than to "sometime".

### Template 2: The Direct Interest

**Subject:** [Job title] role on your team

~~~text
Hi [First name],

I saw [Company] is hiring a [Job title], and it looks like the role is mostly about [top priority from the posting]. That's the work I've been doing for [number] years.

At [Current or last company], I [win with a number]. I also [second win, tied to the second priority].

I've applied through the careers page, and I wanted to reach out to you directly too. Would you be open to a 15-minute call about what you need in this hire?

Thanks,
[Your name]
[Phone] | [LinkedIn URL]
~~~

**When to use it:** There's a posted job, you've found the person who leads the team, and you're a strong match for the top of the posting.

**Make it yours:** Read the first three bullets of the posting. Those are usually the priorities. Pick the wins from your resume that match them most closely, and use the posting's own words where they fit.

### Template 3: The Quick Bump

**Subject:** Reply in the same thread (keep the original subject)

~~~text
Hi [First name],

Bumping this up in case it got buried. I'd still love 15 minutes to hear about [team or role]. If [day] or [day] works, I'll fit around your schedule.

Thanks,
[Your name]
~~~

**When to use it:** 4 to 6 business days after your first email, with no reply.

**Make it yours:** Hit reply on your original email so they see both in one thread. Keep it to three lines. No guilt, no "just checking in".

### Template 4: "Who's the Right Person?"

**Subject:** Quick question about [team] at [Company]

~~~text
Hi [First name],

I'm a [Your title], and I'm interested in [team or type of role] at [Company]. I'm trying to reach whoever leads [team or function], and your name came up when I looked at the team.

If that's you, I'd love 15 minutes to learn what the team needs. If it's someone else, would you mind pointing me to them?

Either way, thanks for reading.

[Your name]
[LinkedIn URL]
~~~

**When to use it:** You can't tell who the hiring manager is, or you've found someone close to the team but not quite the right person.

**Make it yours:** Send this to someone senior enough to know the org chart (a team lead or director). If they point you to a colleague, thank them and mention their name in your next email: "[Name] suggested I reach out."

### Template 5: Answering "How Did You Get My Email?"

~~~text
Hi [First name],

Fair question! I worked it out from [Company]'s email format [or: found it on your website / your speaker bio]. I wanted to reach you directly instead of sending a note into a general inbox.

If you'd rather I go through [the recruiter / the careers page], I'm happy to do that.

Thanks for replying,
[Your name]
~~~

**When to use it:** Someone asks where you found their address. It's rare, but it's nice to have the answer ready.

**Make it yours:** Be honest, be brief, and don't apologize too much. You did something resourceful. If they ask you to use another channel, do exactly that and don't email them again.
