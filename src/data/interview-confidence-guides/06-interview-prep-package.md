# Interview Prep Package

**Everything you fill in before an interview, in one place: your story bank, your question map, the questions you'll ask, a prep sheet for each company and your interview-day checklist.**

When someone tells me "I have an interview on Thursday," the first thing I ask is: "Where are your notes?" Not your resume. Your notes. The stories you'll tell, the questions you'll ask, what you know about this company and this team.

Most people walk in with nothing written down. They wing it, and then spend the drive home thinking of everything they should have said.

This guide is the fix. It's a working package you fill in once (Parts 1 to 3) and then reuse for every interview, plus a one-page prep sheet you fill in fresh for each company (Part 4) and a checklist for the day itself (Part 5).

**Time:** About 2 hours the first time. About 30 minutes per interview after that. **What you'll need:** Your resume, the job post, and your story ideas from Guide 01.

*Results vary. Preparation won't guarantee an offer, but it takes "I froze" off the table.*

---

## How to Use This Package

**When the interview invite lands (same day):**
1. Fill in a Company Prep Sheet (Part 4). Start with the job post.
2. Pick the 3 to 5 stories from your bank that match what this job needs most.
3. Choose 3 to 5 questions to ask (Part 3).

**Two days before:**
1. Practise your "Tell me about yourself" out loud (Guide 03).
2. Say your top stories out loud. Time them.
3. Run a mock interview (the Interview Coach skill in this kit can do this with you).

**The day before and the day of:**
1. Work through the Interview-Day Checklist (Part 5).
2. Use the anxiety protocols in Guide 02.

**Within 24 hours after:**
1. Send your thank-you email (Guide 08).
2. Do the debrief worksheet (Guide 05).

Give every story a short, memorable title ("The Friday outage", "The vendor who wouldn't budge"). In the moment, you'll remember the title faster than the details.

### One Card Per Story

Copy this card for each story:

```
STORY #[Number]: [Short title]
Themes: [Theme 1], [Theme 2], [Theme 3]
Where and when: [Job title] at [Company], [Year]

SITUATION (1 or 2 sentences of context):

TASK (1 sentence: what was on YOU):

ACTION (3 to 5 specific things YOU did. Use "I"):
1.
2.
3.

RESULT (the number, and what changed because of it):

WHAT I LEARNED (for failure and feedback questions):
```

### Dig for the Number

A result with a number sticks. A result without one fades. For every story, ask yourself:

- **How many?** People, customers, projects, tickets, locations.
- **How much?** Dollars saved, revenue, budget, hours.
- **How fast?** Days saved, deadline beaten, turnaround time.
- **Compared with what?** Before and after. Last quarter versus this quarter.
- **Who noticed?** A promotion, an award, a manager asking you to train others.

Don't know the exact number? Look for it in old reports, emails, performance reviews or project files. If you still can't find it, give an honest range ("about 10 hours a week") and be ready to explain how you got it. Never make one up. Interviewers ask follow-up questions, and a number you can't explain does more damage than no number at all.

### Example Card (fictional)

```
STORY #4: The backlog nobody owned
Themes: Initiative, Problem-solving, Process improvement
Where and when: Project Coordinator at a regional logistics company, 2023

SITUATION: Our team had a shared inbox of customer change requests. Nobody owned it,
and requests sat for days.

TASK: I wasn't asked to fix it, but late changes were hitting my projects.

ACTION:
1. I tracked every request for two weeks to see where the delays came from.
2. I built a simple intake form and a daily 10-minute triage with two teammates.
3. I wrote a one-page guide so anyone could cover the inbox when we were away.

RESULT: Average response time went from about 4 days to same-day within a month.
My manager rolled the process out to two other teams.

WHAT I LEARNED: Small, boring fixes can matter more than big projects.
```

Sam, the fictional coordinator above, didn't lead a team or have a fancy title. That's the point. Day-to-day stories are often your best ones.

## Part 2: Map Common Questions to Your Stories

Here's the thing: most behavioural questions are the same 8 to 10 themes wearing different outfits. Once you've mapped your stories, you're never searching your memory under pressure. You're just picking a card.

Fill in the story number you'd use for each question:

| Theme | Common question | My story # |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership | "Tell me about a time you led a team through a challenge." | |
| Leadership | "Describe a time you had to motivate someone who was struggling." | |
| Problem-solving | "Tell me about a complex problem you solved." | |

| Collaboration | "Describe a cross-functional project you worked on." | |
| Conflict | "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager." | |
| Conflict | "Describe a time you received critical feedback." | |
| Improvement | "Tell me about a time you improved a process or system." | |
| Improvement | "Describe a time you came up with a new approach to an old problem." | |
| Failure | "Tell me about a time you failed." | |
| Failure | "Describe a mistake you made and how you handled it." | |
| Prioritization | "Tell me about a time you had to manage multiple priorities." | |
| Prioritization | "Describe how you handled a tight deadline." | |
| Communication | "Tell me about a time you explained something complex to a non-expert." | |
| Communication | "Describe a time you had to deliver bad news." | |
| Customer focus | "Tell me about a time you went out of your way for a customer." | |
| Adaptability | "Describe a time you had to learn something quickly." | |

### Check Your Map

- **Every theme has at least one story.** If a row is empty, go back to the mining prompts in Guide 01.
- **You have at least two failure or feedback stories.** These come up more than people expect.
- **No single story covers more than four rows.** If one story is doing all the work, you'll sound repetitive.
- **Don't repeat a story with the same interviewer.** In a long day of interviews, track which stories you've used with whom.

One story can answer several questions. The difference is where you spend your Action time. Guide 01 shows you how to shift the emphasis.

## Part 3: Questions to Ask the Interviewer

"Do you have any questions for us?" isn't a courtesy. It's part of the interview. Pick 3 to 5 for each conversation, and match them to who you're talking to.

### About the Role (ask the hiring manager)
- "What does success look like in this role in the first 90 days?"
- "What are the biggest challenges facing the team right now?"
- "How does this role fit into the team's plans for this year?"
- "What's the typical career path for someone in this role?"

### About the Team (ask the hiring manager or future teammates)
- "Who would I be working with most closely?"
- "How would you describe the way the team works together?"
- "What do you enjoy most about working on this team?"

### About the Company (ask anyone)
- "How has the company changed in the past year? Where's it headed?"
- "What are the company's top priorities for the next 6 to 12 months?"
- "How does the company support professional development?"

### About the Interviewer (good for building rapport)
- "What brought you to [Company]?"
- "What's been your favourite project here?"
- "What surprised you most when you joined?"

### Deal-Breakers (ask the recruiter, or save for later rounds)
- "What's the team's approach to remote or hybrid work?"
- "What does a typical week look like in terms of hours?"
- "Can you walk me through the performance review process?"

### For Later Rounds
- "What's the biggest risk facing the company right now?"
- "How does leadership make decisions here?"
- "If you could change one thing about how the team works, what would it be?"

### The Closing Question (ask at the very end)

> "Based on our conversation, is there anything about my background that gives you pause?"

This one takes nerve. Ask it anyway. It gives you a chance to clear up a concern while you're still in the room, instead of finding out in a rejection email.

If they name a concern, here's what I want you to do:
1. **Thank them.** "That's fair, thanks for being direct."
2. **Answer with evidence.** Tell a short story or give a fact that speaks to it.
3. **Check.** "Does that help with the concern?"

If you run out of time to address it well, cover it in your thank-you email (Guide 08).

### Skip These
- Anything you could find on their website in two minutes.
- Pay and benefits in a first interview, unless they raise it. The recruiter is the right person for those.
- "Did I get the job?" They can't answer it, and it puts them on the spot.

### Where to Find the Information

- **The job post.** Read it twice. The first few bullet points and anything repeated are usually what they care about most.
- **The company website.** The About page, the news or press page, and the careers page.
- **A news search.** Search the company name and filter to the last month.
- **LinkedIn.** The company page (recent posts, headcount growth), the interviewer's profile, and posts by people on the team.
- **Employee review sites.** Useful for patterns, but read them with a grain of salt. Happy people rarely write reviews.
- **Annual reports or investor pages** (for public companies). The opening letter often names the year's top priorities.

## Part 5: Interview-Day Checklist

### The Day Before
- [ ] Review your Company Prep Sheet
- [ ] Say your top 3 to 5 stories out loud
- [ ] Practise your "Tell me about yourself" (Guide 03)
- [ ] Have 5 to 7 questions ready to ask
- [ ] Video interview: test the link, camera, microphone and lighting
- [ ] In person: plan your route and when you'll leave
- [ ] Lay out what you'll wear
- [ ] In person: print 2 or 3 copies of your resume
- [ ] Set a cut-off time for prep, then do something else (Guide 02)

### The Morning Of
- [ ] Eat something
- [ ] Move your body for 10 minutes
- [ ] Glance at your prep sheet. Don't cram.
- [ ] Water, notepad and pen within reach
- [ ] Video: close extra tabs and turn off notifications
- [ ] Log in 5 minutes early, or arrive at reception 10 minutes early

### During the Interview
- [ ] Smile and make eye contact (on video, look at the camera)
- [ ] Listen to the whole question before you answer. A short pause is fine.
- [ ] Ask them to repeat or clarify if you need to
- [ ] Take brief notes, especially on their challenges and priorities
- [ ] Refer back to things they said earlier
- [ ] Ask your questions, including the closing question
- [ ] Ask about next steps and timing
- [ ] Thank them by name

### Within 24 Hours After
- [ ] Send a personal thank-you email to each interviewer (Guide 08)
- [ ] Fill in the debrief worksheet (Guide 05)
- [ ] Add notes to your job search tracker
- [ ] Send anything they asked for, promptly
- [ ] Set a reminder to follow up (Guide 05 has the timing)
- [ ] Keep applying. Nothing is final until you have a written offer.

---

## Key Takeaway

You don't need to be the most impressive person they interview. You need to be the most prepared one.

Fill in your story bank and question map once. Fill in a prep sheet for every interview. Run the checklist. Then walk in and have a conversation, because the hard work is already done.
