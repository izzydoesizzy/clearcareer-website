# Build Your Profile From Scratch

**A first LinkedIn profile, section by section: strategy, photo, banner, headline, About, experience and skills. For new grads, veterans, career changers and anyone starting over.**

A blank LinkedIn profile is intimidating. Every box wants something, and it's hard to know what "good" looks like when you've never had one.

So let's not do it all at once. This guide walks you through it in the same order I use when I build a first profile with someone: decide what the profile is for, get the photo and banner sorted, then write the headline, About, experience and skills. Every word comes from your real experience.

It works for anyone starting from zero, and I've added notes for three groups who often start here:

- **New grads:** your co-op terms, internships, part-time jobs, volunteer roles and school projects count.
- **Veterans:** your experience is real. It just needs translating into civilian words.
- **Career changers:** lead with where you're going, and back it up with what you've already done that fits.

**Time:** 2 to 3 hours, in one sitting or spread over a few days. **What you'll need:** Your resume (or a list of your roles, school and volunteer work), your target job titles, 3 to 5 wins, a recent photo, and the email you want recruiters to use.

---

## Step 1: Your strategy in one sentence

Before you write anything, decide what this profile is for. Who should find it, and what should they understand about you in the first 5 seconds?

~~~text
I want [who: recruiters, hiring managers, people in my field] looking for [target job title] to see me as [the one thing you want them to remember], because [your proof: a win, your experience, your training].
~~~

My strategy: ________________

For example, Dana is a fictional high school teacher moving into instructional design: "I want hiring managers looking for an instructional designer to see me as someone who builds training people finish, because I've spent 8 years designing lessons for teenagers and 6 other teachers adopted mine."

Every section after this one should back up that sentence. If something doesn't, leave it out.

---

## Step 2: Your profile photo

- **Recent and real.** A photo from the last year or two that looks like you on a video call. If you touch it up with an AI tool, keep it looking like you.
- **Face front and centre.** Head and shoulders, with your face filling most of the frame. No group shots cropped down, no sunglasses.
- **Good light.** Stand facing a window during the day. A phone camera is fine.
- **Plain background.** A wall, a door, some trees. Nothing busy behind you.
- **Dress for the job you want.** What you'd wear to an interview for your target role.

Ask a friend to take 20 shots and pick the best one. It's faster than you think.

---

## Step 3: Your banner

The banner is the wide image behind your photo. Most people leave the default. A simple one is an easy upgrade.

- **Keep it simple.** A plain background with your name and target role, or a short line about what you do, is plenty. Canva has free LinkedIn banner templates.
- **Mind the photo.** Your profile photo covers part of the banner on the left, so keep any text toward the centre or the right.
- **Use the right size.** LinkedIn recommends 1584 by 396 pixels as I write this. Canva's LinkedIn banner templates are already set to the right size.

---

## Step 4: Your headline (3 options)

Use the pipe format from Guide 01: Job Title | Key Experience or Skills | Major Achievement. Up to 220 characters. Write 3 versions, then pick one to start with:

1. **Safe/Direct:** clear, keyword-rich and professional.
2. **Result-Focused:** leads with your strongest result.
3. **Distinctive/Niche:** a specific angle from your most unusual experience.

**Priya, a fictional new grad with a co-op term in accounts payable:**

1. Accounts Payable Analyst | Business Administration Grad | Invoice Processing, Account Reconciliations and Excel (111 characters)
2. Accounts Payable Analyst | Processed 400+ Invoices a Month in My Co-op Term | Cleared a 3-Week Invoice Backlog in 10 Days (121 characters)
3. Accounts Payable Analyst | Business Grad Who Loves a Clean Reconciliation | Co-op Experience at a Regional Construction Firm (124 characters)

**Marcus, a fictional veteran leaving the military after 12 years in logistics:**

1. Logistics and Operations Manager | 12 Years of Military Logistics | Fleet, Warehouse and Team Leadership (104 characters)
2. Logistics and Operations Manager | Led a 30-Person Supply Team | Tracked $8M in Equipment with Zero Losses at Audit (115 characters)
3. Operations Manager | Military Logistics Veteran | I Move People and Equipment on Tight Timelines, in Any Weather (112 characters)

**Dana, a fictional teacher moving into instructional design:**

1. Instructional Designer | Former High School Teacher | Curriculum Design, Google Classroom and Canva (99 characters)
2. Instructional Designer | Built 40+ Lessons Adopted by 6 Teachers | Trained 12 Colleagues to Teach Online in 2 Weeks (115 characters)
3. Instructional Designer | 8 Years Teaching Teenagers at 8:30 a.m., So Adult Learners Don't Scare Me | Canva, Google Classroom (124 characters)

Notice that all three people lead with the job they want, then prove it with what they've done.

My Safe/Direct: ________________

My Result-Focused: ________________

My Distinctive/Niche: ________________

I'll start with: ________________ because ________________

## Step 5: Your About section

For a first profile, I like a short, first-person story: 3 to 5 short paragraphs.

1. **An opening hook.** One line that makes someone want to keep reading.
2. **What you do and for whom.** One or two sentences.
3. **Proof points.** Real numbers and roles. A short list works well here.
4. **What you're looking for now.** Titles, industries, the kind of place.
5. **A call to action with your email.**

Once you have 5 strong wins, you can switch to the 4-part About in Guide 02. Either works.

**Dana's About (fictional example):**

~~~text
I spent 8 years designing lessons for the toughest audience I know: teenagers at 8:30 in the morning.

Now I design learning for adults. I'm an instructional designer who builds short, practical courses that people finish, using what the classroom taught me about attention, practice and feedback.

A few things I've built:
→ 40+ lessons and units that 6 other teachers adopted across my department
→ A Google Classroom training that got 12 colleagues teaching online in 2 weeks
→ Short video tutorials, made in Canva, for 150+ students a year
→ A new reading assessment that I piloted with 3 classes and then rolled out to the whole department

I'm looking for instructional design and learning and development roles at organizations that take training seriously, especially in education technology, health care and the public sector.

If your team needs someone who can turn a 60-page manual into a course people remember, let's talk.

Connect with me at: → dana@example.com
~~~

That's 976 characters. LinkedIn allows up to 2,600 (as I write this), so there's room to grow as you add wins.

## Step 6: Your experience (4 to 5 bullets per role)

Pick your 3 most recent relevant roles. For each one, write 4 to 5 bullets:

~~~text
[Job title] | [Company] | [Dates]
- [Strong verb] + [what you did] + [the result, with a real number if you have one]
~~~

Strong verbs to start with: Managed, Delivered, Reduced, Built, Trained, Coordinated, Processed, Launched, Fixed, Organized.

**Dana's teaching role (fictional example):**

~~~text
High School Teacher | Public School Board, Ontario | 2017 to 2025
- Designed 40+ lessons and units that 6 other teachers adopted across the department
- Trained 12 colleagues on Google Classroom, getting the department teaching online in 2 weeks
- Created short video tutorials in Canva for 150+ students a year
- Piloted a new reading assessment with 3 classes, then rolled it out department-wide
~~~

**If you're a new grad:** your co-op terms, internships, part-time jobs and volunteer roles all go here as real roles. A school project with a real client or a real result can go in the Projects section. Add your school, program and any awards to Education.

**If you're a veteran:** translate ranks, unit names and acronyms into civilian words. Say how many people you led, what you were responsible for (equipment, budget, schedules) and what the outcome was. Leave out anything you can't share publicly. In Canada and the US the terms differ, but the rule is the same: write it so a civilian hiring manager understands it on the first read.

**If you're changing careers:** keep your past roles, but write each bullet around the parts of the work that look most like the new job. Dana's bullets are about designing, training and building, not about marking tests.

**Only use numbers you can explain.** If you estimate, say "about" and know how you got there.

## Step 7: Your skills (12 to 15)

Add 12 to 15 skills, using the exact words that show up in job posts for your target role. Group them so you can check you've covered the basics:

**Dana's skills (fictional example):**

- **Learning design:** Instructional Design, Curriculum Development, Lesson Planning, Assessment Design, Adult Learning
- **Tools:** Canva, Google Classroom, Google Workspace, Video Editing
- **People:** Training Delivery, Coaching, Presentation Skills, Stakeholder Communication

My skills:

Group 1: ________________

Group 2: ________________

Group 3: ________________

Tip: open 3 job posts for your target role and highlight every skill that shows up in more than one. Those go first.

---

## Step 8: Finishing touches

- [ ] **Custom URL:** edit your public profile URL so it's your name (for example linkedin.com/in/firstname-lastname). Put it on your resume.
- [ ] **Location:** set the city you want to work in, or the closest major city.
- [ ] **Contact info:** add the email you put in your About.
- [ ] **Open to Work:** turn it on and pick your job titles and locations. If you're employed, you can show it to recruiters only (LinkedIn can't fully guarantee who sees it).
- [ ] **Education:** school, program and dates if you're comfortable sharing them.
- [ ] **Second language:** in Quebec or another bilingual market, consider adding a French version of your profile. LinkedIn lets you add a profile in a second language.
- [ ] **Check it on your phone.** That's where a lot of people will see it first.

A complete profile makes it easier for the right people to find you and say yes to a conversation. It can't guarantee interviews or offers. Results vary. But it means that when someone looks you up, there's something real to find.
